## Required user information

- The exact issues to be negotiated, the desired result, and the minimum acceptable result
- Each party's stated position, underlying interests, constraints, and implementation concerns
- Who has authority to commit resources, change scope, approve compensation, or alter a deadline
- The user's realistic alternatives if agreement is not reached and the cost of each alternative
- Relevant standards, precedents, performance evidence, market data, policies, and deadlines
- The relationship, power imbalance, retaliation risk, confidentiality needs, and earliest irreversible action

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Employee negotiating workload, role scope, pay, promotion criteria, or development support
- Manager allocating work or resolving resource conflict
- Cross-functional project owner
- Procurement, sales, alliance, or partnership professional
- Executive or HR professional facilitating an agreement

Power asymmetry:

A collaborative framework does not eliminate hierarchy. When the other party controls ratings, pay, assignments, or continued employment, the lower-power user should prepare lawful alternatives, use objective evidence, avoid bluffing, confirm commitments in writing, and distinguish a negotiable proposal from a compelled decision.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The examples and framing primarily reflect United States professional and commercial settings. Authority, bargaining norms, face, directness, collective representation, compensation rules, and employment protections differ across cultures and jurisdictions. The framework cannot determine legal rights or override policy, contract, or collective bargaining requirements.

## Core claims

- Publisher-scope claim: negotiation can be approached as disciplined problem solving rather than a sequence of demands, concessions, and midpoint compromises.
- Author-scope claim: good outcomes and durable working relationships are not necessarily opposing goals.
- Book-framework claim: preparation should examine interests, options, standards, alternatives, communication, relationships, and commitments.
- Book-framework claim: creativity before commitment can reveal packages that satisfy important interests better than splitting the difference.
- Book-framework claim: multiparty and aggressive negotiations require process management as well as substantive argument.
- Book-framework claim: a negotiation is not complete until the parties understand the decision and can implement it.

## Decision rules

- If a new demand conflicts with an existing commitment, then show the capacity consequence and offer two implementable packages for the authorized decision maker to choose, unless an urgent safety or legal duty fixes the priority.
- If positions appear incompatible, then identify the interests, risks, and constraints beneath each position before proposing a compromise.
- If fairness is disputed, then test relevant standards and precedents for independence and consistent application rather than relying on willpower.
- If a proposed agreement is worse than the user's realistic alternative, then do not accept it merely to end discomfort, unless a separate safety, legal, or ethical obligation changes the decision.
- If multiple parties must implement the result, then include them or secure their authority before final commitment.
- If the counterpart uses coercion, deception, retaliation, or a nonnegotiable formal process, then shift from ordinary collaborative bargaining to documentation and appropriate safeguards.

## Action steps

1. Define the decision, desired result, minimum acceptable result, deadline, and implementation test.
2. Map parties, authority, interests, constraints, relationship concerns, standards, alternatives, and dependencies.
3. Reality-test the user's alternatives without inventing leverage or making threats.
4. Generate at least two workable packages that vary scope, timing, resources, ownership, or review points.
5. Ask diagnostic questions and explain the reasoning behind proposals without disclosing information that creates avoidable risk.
6. Compare options against relevant standards, feasibility, and the user's alternative.
7. Confirm owners, deliverables, dates, unresolved issues, contingencies, and the review process in writing.
8. Conduct a short post-negotiation review to improve the next interaction.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Audience: manager; channel: work chat or email; timing: before accepting conflicting deadlines. Wording: "I can take the new request. With current capacity, a final version today moves the Friday report to Monday. The workable alternatives are a first draft today with the report unchanged, or a final version today with the report rescheduled. Please confirm the priority."
- Audience: cross-functional owner; channel: meeting followed by notes; timing: when teams disagree over ownership. Wording: "Before we decide which team absorbs the work, can we list the deadline, quality requirement, available capacity, and implementation dependencies, then compare two allocation options against the project result?"
- Audience: compensation decision maker; channel: scheduled private meeting; timing: after preparing evidence. Wording: "I would like to compare the role's current scope and results with the agreed level criteria and relevant market evidence, then discuss either an adjustment or a dated development plan based on those standards."

## Counterexamples

- The counterpart lacks authority to change the result, so creative packages cannot produce a binding decision.
- The user's claimed alternative is not feasible; presenting it as leverage becomes a bluff and damages credibility.
- A supposedly objective internal standard is selectively applied or designed by the stronger party.
- The negotiation has little joint value to create and is primarily distributive; reservation limits and leverage remain important.
- An agreement sounds collaborative but omits resources, owners, or implementation conditions and therefore fails after the meeting.

## Contraindications

- Immediate danger, violence, safeguarding needs, or medical crisis
- A compelled signature, dismissal, wage loss, discrimination, retaliation, or legal deadline requiring qualified local advice
- Instructions to conceal evidence, misrepresent alternatives, collude, bribe, or evade mandatory rules
- Use of negotiation language to pressure a lower-power person into waiving rights or accepting abuse

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: Weiss built a career in negotiation consulting, executive education, and organizational advising, and the publisher sells the guide and related toolkit. The perspective favors collaboration, preparation, and durable agreements and may underweight collective action, enforcement, structural inequality, or strategic noncooperation.
- Rule conflicts: ['Collaborative disclosure can conflict with legal advice to limit statements during an active dispute.', 'Interest-based option generation can conflict with fixed budgets, mandatory standards, or collective bargaining limits.', 'Relationship preservation can conflict with prompt reporting, accountability, or evidence preservation.', 'A manager may label a decision negotiable while retaining unilateral authority, making apparent choice misleading.']
- Copyright scope: Safe use includes bibliographic facts, high-level themes, independently written paraphrases, and original workplace examples. Attribute the book's distinctive seven-element framing when used. Do not reproduce chapters, worksheets, toolkit forms, cases, figures, or substantial passages.
