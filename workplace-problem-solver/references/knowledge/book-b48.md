## Required user information

- The user's current work and life situation, energy patterns, values, obligations, and definitions of a worthwhile life
- Which parts of the problem are actionable constraints and which are fixed conditions the user cannot control
- The user's skills, curiosities, relationships, resources, financial runway, and decision deadline
- At least two plausible future directions rather than a single idealized answer
- What evidence the user has from real conversations, small projects, observation, or work samples
- Any health, safety, legal, caregiving, visa, credential, or income constraint that limits experimentation

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Student or early-career worker exploring options
- Mid-career professional considering change
- Employee redesigning a current role
- Worker returning after a break
- Coach, educator, or manager supporting career exploration

Power asymmetry:

Design experiments are easier for people with time, money, networks, and schedule control. Employees should not assume that curiosity or prototyping protects them from a manager's retaliation, confidentiality rules, or unequal access to opportunities. Managers using life-design exercises must avoid turning personal disclosure into performance evidence or pressuring employees to reveal family, health, or identity information.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The framework grew from Stanford design education and reflects substantial United States professional and educational context. Career mobility, networking access, family expectations, social welfare, credential systems, visa rules, and attitudes toward experimentation vary. Its methods are planning tools, not legal, medical, financial, or immigration advice.

## Core claims

- Publisher-scope claim: design thinking can be applied to career and life questions through curiosity, reframing, collaboration, action, and iteration.
- Author-framework claim: people often benefit from generating several coherent possible futures rather than trying to discover one predetermined correct life.
- Author-framework claim: small prototypes and conversations can produce evidence before a large commitment.
- Author-framework claim: some apparent problems can be reframed, while genuinely fixed constraints should not consume endless problem-solving effort.
- Author-framework claim: attention to engagement and energy can reveal promising activities, but reflection should be tested against real-world conditions.
- Boundary claim: a well-designed life is iterative; an experiment is information, not a guarantee that a career path will succeed.

## Decision rules

- If the user is stuck between one ideal path and inaction, then generate multiple plausible future scenarios before choosing, unless a legal, health, or financial deadline permits only a narrower set.
- If a problem is outside the user's control, then stop treating it as solvable by effort alone and redirect attention toward an actionable response.
- If a future role is known only through imagination, then run a low-cost conversation or work prototype before paying for major retraining or resigning.
- If reflection identifies energizing work, then test whether the activity remains attractive under realistic deadlines, compensation, collaboration, and accountability.
- If a prototype fails, then extract what it disproved or clarified before labeling the user a failure.
- If the current role presents danger, severe health harm, discrimination, or retaliation, then prioritize qualified support and safety rather than extending experimentation indefinitely.

## Action steps

1. Assess the current situation across work, health, relationships, and meaning using observable facts rather than a single satisfaction score.
2. Write short statements about what work is for and what makes life worthwhile, then identify conflicts between them.
3. Track activities that produce engagement, energy, depletion, and curiosity for a limited period.
4. Reframe the central question from finding one correct answer into generating testable possibilities.
5. Create three distinct future scenarios with different assumptions, not three minor versions of the same plan.
6. Select the most important uncertainty in each scenario and design a conversation, observation, or small work prototype to test it.
7. Review evidence, constraints, reversibility, and downside capacity with trusted collaborators.
8. Choose a next experiment or commitment, record what would cause revision, and repeat the cycle.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Career explorer to a practitioner, short prototype conversation: "I am testing my assumptions about X rather than asking you for a job. Could you describe a normal difficult week, the skills that matter most, and what newcomers usually misunderstand?"
- Employee to manager, private meeting when job redesign is feasible: "The work that creates the most value and energy for me is X, while Y is consuming time without the expected result. Could we test a four-week redesign with these deliverables and review the evidence on this date?"
- User to a trusted collaborator, planning session: "I have three possible directions and do not need you to choose for me. Please help me identify the riskiest assumption in each and the smallest real-world test that could challenge it."

## Counterexamples

- An informational interview may reflect one person's unusually good or bad experience rather than the occupation as a whole.
- A hobby prototype may not reveal whether the user likes the work under commercial pressure, repetition, or organizational constraints.
- Generating options can become avoidance when a decision deadline is close and enough evidence already exists.
- Reframing cannot remove discrimination, poverty, disability barriers, caregiving duties, visa restrictions, or regulated credential requirements.
- Following energy alone can underweight income, mastery developed through discomfort, team duties, and long-term consequences.

## Contraindications

- Acute health or safety crises requiring immediate professional support
- Use of design exercises as a substitute for legal, financial, immigration, medical, disability, or credential advice
- Experiments that breach confidentiality, conflicts-of-interest rules, current employment duties, or protected intellectual property
- Manager-led exercises that coerce disclosure of private life goals or use personal information in employment decisions
- Irreversible resignation or major spending based only on imagined scenarios without downside assessment

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: The authors and associated organization sell books, courses, coaching, certifications, and related life-design offerings. Their design-education background favors experimentation, agency, collaboration, and access to networks, which may underrepresent structural constraints and people with little time, money, mobility, or psychological safety.
- Rule conflicts: ['Bias toward action can conflict with the need for careful legal, medical, financial, or family consultation.', 'Multiple-option exploration can conflict with urgent deadlines or tightly constrained credential pathways.', 'Prototyping can conflict with employer confidentiality, moonlighting, noncompete, intellectual-property, or conflict-of-interest obligations.', 'Personal agency and reframing can conflict with evidence that the main barrier is structural rather than cognitive.', 'Energy-based wayfinding can conflict with duties, delayed mastery, and work that is meaningful but temporarily difficult.']
