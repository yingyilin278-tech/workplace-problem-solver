## Required user information

- The document's business purpose and the decision, response, or behavior sought from the reader
- The intended readers, their knowledge, interests, objections, authority, available time, and likely reading channel
- The document type, such as email, proposal, report, memo, letter, or performance appraisal
- The verified facts, evidence, dates, numbers, owners, and deadlines that must be communicated
- The required tone, organizational conventions, confidentiality level, approval process, and legal or regulatory constraints
- Whether the user needs drafting, structural revision, line editing, proofreading, or a final-risk review

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Employee writing routine workplace messages and reports
- Manager communicating decisions, expectations, and performance information
- Executive writing for senior stakeholders or external audiences
- HR professional preparing sensitive employee communications
- Analyst, consultant, salesperson, lawyer, or project lead producing persuasive business documents

Power asymmetry:

Clear writing can reduce ambiguity but cannot remove hierarchy. A lower-power writer should distinguish a request from a decision, avoid claiming authority they do not have, and preserve factual records when commitments, performance, or accountability are involved. A manager's polished wording can still be coercive if it hides consequences or denies meaningful choice. Sensitive upward or employee-facing messages may require review by an authorized owner.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The guidance is rooted in contemporary American English business practice. Directness, informality, reader address, email conventions, punctuation, and persuasive ordering vary across languages, high-context cultures, public-sector settings, and regulated professions. Local law and policy control notices, employment records, accessibility, privacy, contracting, and required disclosures.

## Core claims

- Publisher-scope claim: business writing is a practical influence skill because unclear emails, proposals, and reports consume time and weaken credibility.
- Author-method claim: define the purpose and understand the reader before choosing structure, detail, tone, or wording.
- Author-method claim: separating idea generation, organization, drafting, and editing reduces writer's block and prevents premature polishing.
- Author-method claim: a small number of complete-sentence propositions can provide the architecture for a longer document.
- Author-method claim: plain, concise, concrete language generally makes strong ideas more credible than inflated diction or unnecessary jargon.
- Practice-scope claim: revision should improve substance, order, clarity, tone, and correctness rather than merely fix grammar.
- Boundary claim: good prose cannot repair false evidence, an unauthorized commitment, a discriminatory standard, or a legally defective process.

## Decision rules

- If the writer cannot state the desired reader response in one sentence, then clarify purpose before drafting, unless an emergency requires a short factual alert first.
- If readers differ materially in knowledge or authority, then identify the primary decision maker and provide layered detail rather than writing one undifferentiated block.
- If drafting stalls because the writer is editing every sentence, then separate brainstorming, ordering, drafting, and revision into distinct passes.
- If a document contains several ideas, then write the main propositions as complete sentences and arrange them before expanding the prose.
- If readers need to act quickly, then lead with the conclusion, request, owner, and deadline, unless bad-news, legal, or cultural context requires a more carefully staged opening.
- If a sentence uses jargon, abstraction, or inflated wording without adding precision, then replace it with shorter concrete language.
- If the document creates a formal employment, legal, financial, safety, or regulatory record, then obtain qualified review rather than relying on style guidance alone.

## Action steps

1. Write a one-sentence purpose statement naming the audience and the response required.
2. List what the reader already knows, what they need to know, what may concern them, and what evidence supports the message.
3. Brainstorm the necessary content without editing it.
4. Reduce the material to two or three complete-sentence propositions and order them by reader need.
5. Draft rapidly from that structure, using concrete subjects, active verbs where accurate, and explicit owners and dates.
6. Revise first for logic and missing evidence, then for paragraph order, sentence clarity, tone, concision, and mechanics.
7. Test the subject line, opening, request, deadline, attachment references, and likely interpretation with an uninvolved reader when stakes are material.
8. Preserve the approved version and source evidence when the writing documents commitments, performance, or responsibility.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Employee to manager, email before a decision deadline: "Decision needed by Thursday at 3:00 p.m.: please choose Option A, which preserves the launch date, or Option B, which adds the requested feature and moves launch to May 20. The attached comparison shows cost and delivery effects."
- Project lead to cross-functional partners, written recap immediately after a meeting: "We agreed that Maya owns the client estimate by Tuesday, Chen validates tax and freight by Wednesday, and I send the final quote Thursday. Please reply by noon tomorrow if any owner or date is incorrect."
- Manager to employee, private written follow-up after a performance conversation: "The expected standard is a complete cost check before every quote. The last two quotes omitted freight and implementation labor. For the next three quotes, use the attached checklist and send it with your draft for review."

## Counterexamples

- A very short email can still fail when it omits the evidence, owner, deadline, or consequence that the reader needs.
- Leading with a blunt conclusion can damage trust when the message concerns bereavement, termination, harassment, or another context requiring procedural and human care.
- Active voice is not automatically clearer when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, legally protected, or less important than the process.
- Plain language does not justify oversimplifying technical risk, uncertainty, statistical limitations, or contractual obligations.
- A polished performance appraisal can still be unfair if it relies on recency bias, vague traits, unequal standards, or undocumented claims.

## Contraindications

- Use as a substitute for legal, regulatory, HR, safety, accessibility, or subject-matter review
- Writing that fabricates evidence, conceals a material fact, or implies authority the sender does not possess
- Automated simplification of protected, confidential, medical, or legally privileged information without authorized controls
- Use of persuasive technique to pressure an employee into an irreversible decision or waive rights

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: Garner sells books, courses, consulting, and editorial expertise related to writing and legal language. His prescriptive, American-English orientation may privilege professional norms associated with law, management, and educated knowledge work. The framework may underweight multilingual writers, disability access, organizational politics, and settings where form or technical completeness matters more than elegance.
- Rule conflicts: ['Conciseness can conflict with due-process, disclosure, documentation, or accessibility requirements.', 'Reader-centered persuasion can conflict with duties of neutrality, balanced risk disclosure, or scientific uncertainty.', 'Plain-language preferences can conflict with defined technical or legal terms that must remain exact.', 'A strong direct opening can conflict with high-context cultural norms or sensitive bad-news procedures.', 'A four-stage individual writing process may not fit collaborative documents with controlled templates, versioning, and multiple approvers.']
- Copyright scope: Safe use includes bibliographic facts, high-level writing principles, original examples, and independently expressed summaries of the staged writing process. Do not reproduce chapters, extended examples, checklists, exercises, tables, or distinctive passages from the book or legal preview.
