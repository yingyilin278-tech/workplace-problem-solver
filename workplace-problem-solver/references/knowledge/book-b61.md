## Required user information

- Whether the group is an intact team with shared work, interdependence, and a common result
- The team's purpose, decision rights, roles, priorities, measures, and meeting structure
- Observable evidence of withheld information, unproductive conflict, ambiguous decisions, missed commitments, or status competition
- The leader's behavior, formal hierarchy, retaliation risk, and treatment of dissent or mistakes
- Individual capability, workload, incentives, resources, psychological safety, and cross-team dependencies
- Whether conflict concerns ideas, relationships, discrimination, harassment, safety, or ethics
- The outcome and review period for any team intervention

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Executive team
- Manager and intact work team
- Project or leadership team with shared outcomes
- Team coach, facilitator, or organizational-development professional
- HR or people leader supporting team effectiveness

Power asymmetry:

The model asks for vulnerability, conflict, peer accountability, and result focus, but these behaviors are not equally safe when leaders punish mistakes or control careers. Leaders must demonstrate vulnerability, protect dissent, clarify decision rights, and prevent peer accountability from becoming bullying or surveillance. Lower-power employees should not be required to disclose personal information as proof of trust.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The model comes from United States executive consulting and uses direct discussion, individual voice, and a fictional corporate leadership team. Trust, conflict, commitment, hierarchy, face, consensus, and peer correction vary across cultures. Employment rights, discrimination, disability, privacy, union representation, and professional duties must be handled separately.

## Core claims

- Author-model claim: absence of vulnerability-based trust makes productive teamwork difficult.
- Author-model claim: fear of ideological conflict creates artificial harmony and weakens decisions.
- Author-model claim: insufficient clarity or buy-in produces weak commitment.
- Author-model claim: interpersonal discomfort can prevent peers from holding one another accountable for agreed behavior and performance.
- Author-model claim: individual status or departmental goals can displace shared team results.
- Boundary claim: the five-part model is a practical consulting framework and leadership fable, not a comprehensive or validated diagnosis of every team problem.

## Decision rules

- If team members hide mistakes, uncertainty, or requests for help because they fear punishment, then improve leader behavior and psychological safety before demanding more vulnerability.
- If disagreement concerns ideas and the setting is safe, then surface the competing evidence and decision criteria, unless the issue requires a formal conduct or legal process.
- If a decision follows debate, then record the decision, owner, rationale, dissent, deadline, and review condition before asking for commitment.
- If peers avoid accountability, then confirm that expectations, resources, authority, and comparable standards are clear before encouraging direct correction.
- If individual or departmental incentives conflict with team outcomes, then correct the measures and decision system rather than relying on team spirit alone.

## Action steps

1. Confirm that the group is a real team and define the shared result.
2. Collect confidential examples and outcome data before assigning a dysfunction.
3. Assess leadership behavior, safety, roles, incentives, workload, skills, and external constraints.
4. Select one bottleneck and run a small team practice or meeting change.
5. Record decisions, owners, deadlines, standards, and review points.
6. Invite disagreement and feedback without compelling personal disclosure.
7. Measure behavior and results, then revise or escalate structural issues.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Leader to team, meeting opening: "I may have contributed to this problem by changing priorities without closing decisions. Today I want us to surface the risks, decide, record the owner, and review the result on this date."
- Team member during debate: "I support the shared goal and disagree with this approach for these evidence-based reasons. What information would resolve the difference?"
- Team member to peer, private accountability conversation: "We agreed on X by this date, and the current result is Y, which affects Z. What blocked it, and what commitment or escalation should we record now?"
- Facilitator to leader before a workshop: "Before asking the team for vulnerability, please confirm what information is voluntary, how dissent will be protected, and how you will respond to mistakes."

## Counterexamples

- A team can trust one another and still fail because strategy, skills, staffing, technology, or incentives are inadequate.
- Frequent conflict can be destructive rather than productive when it becomes personal, repetitive, or coercive.
- Commitment after debate can suppress legitimate new evidence if leaders treat every revision as disloyalty.
- Peer accountability can become scapegoating or informal discipline without authority and due process.
- A focus on collective results can conceal unethical metrics, unequal workload, or harm transferred to customers and other teams.

## Contraindications

- Active harassment, discrimination, retaliation, violence, fraud, or safety concerns requiring formal qualified processes
- Forced personal disclosure, humiliation, or personality labeling presented as vulnerability work
- Use of peer accountability to bypass managerial responsibility, policy, representation, or due process
- Application to a loose network or working group without shared outcomes and interdependence
- Use of the model as a substitute for technical diagnosis, staffing, governance, accessibility, or legal compliance

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: Lencioni and The Table Group sell books, assessments, facilitator guides, workshops, consulting, and related products built around the model. This provides extensive implementation experience but creates commercial and confirmation bias. The framework may overemphasize executive behavior and interpersonal dynamics while underweighting structure, expertise, labor conditions, diversity, and external dependencies.
- Rule conflicts: ['Vulnerability-based trust can conflict with privacy, trauma-informed practice, cultural norms, and power-aware psychological safety.', 'Productive conflict can conflict with consensus, professional hierarchy, or safety protocols in some settings.', 'Commitment to a decision can conflict with whistleblowing, ethical dissent, and updating after new evidence.', 'Peer accountability can conflict with formal management responsibility and due process.', 'Team-result focus can conflict with broader organizational, public, customer, safety, or ethical outcomes.']
- Copyright scope: Safe use includes bibliographic facts, names of the five public model elements, high-level explanations, and independently written scripts and procedures. Do not reproduce the fable, assessment, pyramid graphic, exercises, facilitator materials, or substantial passages.
