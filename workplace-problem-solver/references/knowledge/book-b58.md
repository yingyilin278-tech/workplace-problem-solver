## Required user information

- The specific vulnerable act involved: belonging, asking, learning, contributing, dissenting, reporting, or challenging
- What happened afterward, how often, who had authority, and whether there was punishment, exclusion, or retaliation
- Team membership, role clarity, decision rights, performance standards, and accountability mechanisms
- Whether the pattern is interpersonal, managerial, structural, discriminatory, or a formal safety or compliance issue
- Available channels for feedback, escalation, anonymous reporting, employee representation, or protected review
- The user's goal, urgency, health impact, and the earliest irreversible or dangerous step

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- People manager
- Team leader
- Executive
- Cross-functional project lead
- HR, culture, or organizational-development professional
- Employee assessing whether it is safe to speak, learn, contribute, or challenge

Power asymmetry:

Psychological safety is most consequential where leaders can reward or punish vulnerability. Invitations to speak are insufficient when the leader controls ratings, workload, access, or employment and has punished dissent before. Leaders must change observable responses, provide air cover, protect confidentiality, and maintain fair accountability and formal reporting routes.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The framework is designed as broadly applicable but comes from English-language organizational consulting and United States management practice. Belonging, authenticity, public disagreement, mistake disclosure, hierarchy, privacy, protected speech, employee consultation, and reporting duties differ across cultures, sectors, and jurisdictions. Formal legal and safety obligations take priority.

## Core claims

- Author-framework claim: psychological safety can be understood as a culture in which vulnerable acts are rewarded rather than punished.
- Author-framework claim: the framework has four progressive stages: inclusion safety, learner safety, contributor safety, and challenger safety.
- Author-framework claim: inclusion safety addresses belonging; learner safety supports questions, feedback, experimentation, and mistakes; contributor safety supports meaningful contribution with autonomy; challenger safety supports good-faith dissent and improvement.
- Author-framework claim: respect and permission are key drivers across the stages.
- Author-framework claim: high intellectual friction can coexist with low social friction when teams challenge ideas without punishing people.
- Boundary claim: a four-stage diagnostic is not proof that a team is legally compliant, equitable, free from retaliation, or effective.

## Decision rules

- If a person is excluded or demeaned because of identity or membership, then address inclusion and formal equity obligations before asking for more participation.
- If people hide mistakes or avoid questions, then change leaders' responses to uncertainty and error before demanding faster learning.
- If employees have skills but cannot contribute, then clarify roles, provide access and appropriate autonomy, and remove gatekeeping before judging engagement.
- If good-faith dissent is invited but punished, then establish visible protection, decision rules, and follow-up before running another speak-up exercise.
- If accountability is missing, then add clear standards and consequences without using humiliation, retaliation, or silence as control.
- If the issue involves harassment, discrimination, safety, fraud, or protected reporting, then use formal safeguards and qualified review rather than treating it as a culture workshop.

## Action steps

1. Identify which vulnerable behavior is being withheld and what response people reasonably expect.
2. Collect dated examples of invitations, responses, exclusion, learning behavior, contribution, and challenge.
3. Assess respect, permission, role clarity, decision authority, accountability, and retaliation risk.
4. Choose one observable leader behavior to stop and one to start at the relevant stage.
5. State the rule for raising questions, reporting mistakes, offering ideas, and challenging decisions, including protected escalation routes.
6. Run a small test, record whether the response changed, and invite confidential feedback.
7. Escalate structural, discriminatory, safety, or employment issues to the appropriate formal owner.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Leader at a team meeting before discussing risk: "I want concerns before commitment. Please challenge the assumptions, not the person. I will record each concern, the decision owner, and what happens next, and there will be no penalty for a good-faith dissenting view."
- Manager after an employee reports a mistake, in private and promptly: "Thank you for raising it early. Let us separate learning from accountability: first we will contain the impact and understand the conditions, then agree on the correction without hiding the record."
- Employee to a project lead in writing after repeated dismissal: "I raised X on these dates because it affects Y. Please confirm the decision owner, the response to the evidence, and the route for further review if the risk remains unresolved."

## Counterexamples

- A team may score highly on comfort and still avoid rigorous disagreement or accountability.
- Inviting candor can increase danger when leaders retaliate or confidentiality cannot be protected.
- Inclusion language can pressure employees to reveal personal identities or experiences they prefer to keep private.
- Tolerance of every mistake can weaken safety and quality when negligence, deception, or repeated preventable behavior is not addressed.
- A workshop cannot overcome incentives that reward silence, impossible deadlines, or executive misconduct.

## Contraindications

- Use of a team workshop as the sole response to harassment, discrimination, violence, retaliation, fraud, or safety incidents
- Forced vulnerability, identity disclosure, personal storytelling, confession, or public discussion of confidential matters
- Replacement of due process, investigation, whistleblower protection, accommodation, or legal compliance with a culture score
- Use of psychological-safety language to excuse poor performance standards, deception, or dangerous conduct
- Public challenges where credible retaliation or personal danger requires a protected channel

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: Clark founded LeaderFactor, which sells books, courses, consulting, certifications, assessments, and access products based on the Four Stages framework. This creates a commercial incentive to present the model as universal, sequential, measurable, and central to performance; independent validation and alternative models should be considered.
