# B84 — Permission to Feel

## Source status

- Enablement level: L2, pending case validation
- Author: Marc Brackett, psychologist and director of the Yale Center for Emotional Intelligence
- Source type: Research-based book on emotion awareness and regulation
- Coverage: C3, C5, C10, C12

Official sources:

- https://www.marcbrackett.com/permission-to-feel
- https://www.hachettebookgroup.com/titles/marc-brackett/permission-to-feel/9780316537902/

## Required user information

- The emotional triggers in the current situation and the user's label for the feeling.
- The intensity of the feeling on a 0-100 scale.
- The desired outcome and the time horizon.
- The user's history of how they typically handle this feeling.

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Employees processing difficult feedback, conflict, or change.
- Managers supporting emotional regulation in their team.
- Educators and parents within their scope of practice.

Power asymmetry:

Naming and regulating emotions should never be used to suppress legitimate complaints about discrimination, harassment, or unsafe conditions.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The framework is broadly transferable, but local norms about emotional expression vary. Adapt the language to the user's context.

## Core claims

- Emotions carry data; naming them accurately is a skill that improves decisions and relationships.
- The RULER framework: Recognize, Understand, Label, Express, Regulate.
- Suppressing or ignoring emotions does not eliminate them; it often intensifies them.
- Emotion regulation is a learnable skill with documented workplace benefits.

## Decision rules

- If a feeling is intense (above 70/100), pause before acting.
- If the user cannot name the feeling, look for physical cues and the situation's context.
- If the feeling is masking a structural problem (e.g., injustice), the structural fix is required.
- If the user wants to change a feeling, first determine whether the situation should be changed instead.

## Action steps

1. Notice the physical cues associated with the feeling.
2. Name the feeling as specifically as possible.
3. Identify what the feeling is signaling about a need or a value.
4. Choose an expression channel that matches the context.
5. Use a regulation strategy that fits the situation: breath, reframe, action, or seeking support.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Audience: self or coach; channel: private note; timing: at peak feeling. Wording: "我现在感觉 [具体情绪]，强度大约 [0-100]。这件事对我重要的是 [价值/需求]。"
- Audience: team; channel: meeting; timing: when tension rises. Wording: "我注意到讨论让不少人紧绷。我们先停一下，确认我们在说的是同一件事，再继续。"

## Counterexamples

- Do not use emotion regulation to suppress legitimate complaints.
- Do not require people to name feelings in contexts where disclosure is unsafe.
- Do not label the user's feeling for them in a way that minimizes or redirects.

## Contraindications

- Active crisis requiring professional mental-health intervention.
- Workplace situations that are themselves harmful and require escalation, not regulation.

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: Author is a researcher and program developer; framework is widely adopted in schools.
- Rule conflicts: ['Emotion regulation should not be used to silence dissent.', 'Cultural norms about emotional expression differ.']
- Copyright scope: Summarize RULER. Do not reproduce extended passages or worksheets.
