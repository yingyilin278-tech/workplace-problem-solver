## Required user information

- The team's purpose, deliverables, decision rights, membership, duration, and stakeholders
- Each member's role, expertise, workload, dependencies, and authority
- The specific conflict, delay, accountability gap, or harmful behavior and its work impact
- Existing team norms, meeting process, escalation route, and prior attempts to resolve the issue
- Whether the team is stable, temporary, cross-functional, remote, or operating under crisis conditions

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Team leader
- Project manager
- Cross-functional lead
- People manager
- Team member facilitating a working agreement
- HR or organizational-development partner

Power asymmetry:

Team leaders control goals, membership, meetings, visibility, and accountability, while cross-functional leads may have responsibility without authority. Use transparent decision rights, equal access to information, safe dissent, and formal escalation when a powerful member can retaliate or block work.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The book reflects mainstream United States management practice across organizational examples. Participation, disagreement, accountability, hierarchy, and public correction norms vary. Team practice must be adapted to local employment rules, collective agreements, accessibility needs, language, time zones, and cultural expectations.

## Core claims

- Publisher-scope claim: effective teams require deliberate leadership and do not emerge automatically from assembling capable individuals.
- Publisher-scope claim: team leaders should select appropriate members, set clear goals, build cooperation, maintain focus, and hold people accountable.
- Publisher-scope claim: structured conversations and practical exercises can improve the people side of teamwork.
- Publisher-scope claim: leaders need to address harmful behavior before it becomes normalized.
- Boundary claim: team-process interventions cannot solve missing authority, impossible staffing, discrimination, or incompatible executive incentives by themselves.

## Decision rules

- If team members disagree about priorities, then clarify the shared goal, decision owner, criteria, and deadline before debating preferred solutions.
- If cross-functional work stalls, then make dependencies, owners, handoff standards, and escalation paths explicit.
- If a member repeatedly misses commitments, then check clarity, capacity, access, and authority before concluding that motivation is the problem.
- If behavior harms participation or trust, then address the specific behavior and impact promptly, unless safety or retaliation risk requires a protected channel.
- If the team lacks the necessary expertise or decision authority, then change membership or sponsorship rather than demanding more effort from the same group.

## Action steps

1. Write a one-sentence team purpose and define the measurable deliverable.
2. Map roles, decision rights, dependencies, required expertise, and stakeholder approvals.
3. Create a working agreement for communication, meetings, dissent, ownership, and escalation.
4. Use structured agendas that identify the decision, preparation, facilitator, and follow-up.
5. Address missed commitments and harmful behavior with facts, impact, context, and a requested correction.
6. Review team health and outcomes at a fixed checkpoint.
7. Escalate structural, resource, safety, policy, or employment problems to the appropriate owner.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Team leader at project launch: "Our shared outcome is X by this date. These are the decision owners, dependencies, and escalation points. Please identify any responsibility you cannot fulfill with the current authority or capacity."
- Cross-functional lead after a missed handoff: "The agreed input was due on Tuesday and the downstream review could not start. What constraint did we miss, and who will own the revised handoff and date?"
- Team leader addressing behavior privately after a meeting: "When the speaker was interrupted repeatedly, two risks were not heard. In future meetings, please let the owner complete the briefing before challenging the recommendation."

## Counterexamples

- Clear team goals do not resolve conflict when senior leaders reward functions for incompatible outcomes.
- Holding a person accountable is unfair when ownership, access, workload, or decision authority was never established.
- More team-building activities can waste time when the real problem is missing resources or executive sponsorship.
- Immediate behavioral correction can expose a low-power reporter when retaliation safeguards are absent.
- Consensus can weaken accountability or delay a decision that has a legitimate single owner.

## Contraindications

- Harassment, discrimination, violence, retaliation, fraud, or safety incidents requiring formal procedures
- Use of team norms to suppress protected dissent, whistleblowing, accommodation, or lawful collective activity
- Performance or employment action without individual evidence, fair process, and applicable review
- Confidential investigations where open team discussion could compromise participants or evidence

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: The author and Harvard Business Review Press offer teaching, books, and related toolkit products. The leader-centered framing may overestimate a local team leader's authority and underweight structural incentives, labor rights, executive politics, or informal status differences.
- Rule conflicts: ['Leader-led clarity can conflict with participatory governance or employee consultation requirements.', 'Rapid accountability can conflict with disability accommodation, workload evidence, or due process.', 'Cohesion-building can conflict with dissent, minority viewpoints, and psychological safety.', 'Team-level fixes can conflict with evidence that the problem originates in organization design or senior leadership.']
- Copyright scope: Safe use includes bibliographic facts, publisher-listed themes, and independently written team-process rules. Do not reproduce exercises, structured-conversation guides, toolkit worksheets, chapters, examples, or substantial passages.
