# B78 — An Everyone Culture

- Enablement level: L2, pending case validation
- Author: Robert Kegan and Lisa Laskow Lahey, developmental psychologists and faculty at Harvard Graduate School of Education
- Source type: Research-based book on adult development and organizational culture
- Coverage: C5, C10, C12

Official sources:

## Required user information

- Whether the user is in a leadership, peer, or follower role.
- The current team's tolerance for candor and the risk of retaliation.
- The user's growth edges and the feedback they have been avoiding.
- Organizational safeguards for speaking up.

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Senior leaders designing culture and feedback systems.
- Managers running team development sessions.
- Individual contributors practicing developmental self-reflection.

Power asymmetry:

Developmental cultures can be empowering but can also pressure people to disclose more than is safe in low-trust or high-retaliation environments. Apply the model only with appropriate psychological safety and procedural protections.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The model assumes a learning-oriented workplace with HR safeguards. It is not a substitute for labor rights, anti-retaliation policy, or formal grievance procedures.

## Core claims

- Adults continue to develop psychologically across the lifespan.
- Most workplace problems involve a gap between current capability and the demands of the role.
- Deliberate practices—giving and receiving candid feedback, identifying competing commitments, and running small experiments—can close that gap.
- High-performance and personal growth can be pursued simultaneously when the culture treats development as a shared responsibility.

## Decision rules

- If a recurring behavior is undermining performance, treat it as a developmental edge rather than a character flaw.
- If the user cannot identify the competing commitment behind the behavior, ask what the behavior is protecting.
- If speaking up is unsafe, defer the developmental conversation to a safer channel or external support.
- If the team's candor rituals are being used to perform vulnerability rather than to change behavior, redesign the rituals.

## Action steps

1. Identify one specific behavior the user wants to change.
2. Surface the underlying assumption and the competing commitment that keeps the behavior in place.
3. Design a small experiment that tests a new behavior for one to two weeks.
4. Request feedback from a trusted colleague or coach.
5. Review the result and decide whether to continue, adapt, or abandon the experiment.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Audience: self or coach; channel: private note; timing: weekly review. Wording: "这个行为在保护我什么？继续这么做，是在保护什么价值或身份？"
- Audience: team; channel: one-on-one; timing: at the start of a project. Wording: "我们这次的目标里有一项是新尝试。我会请你在一周后给我一个具体反馈，而不是等到结果出错。"

## Counterexamples

- Do not pressure disclosure in environments where retaliation is likely.
- Do not label behaviors as "developmental edges" to avoid accountability.
- Do not use the framework to bypass formal HR or legal procedures for serious issues.

## Contraindications

- Active retaliation, harassment, or discrimination claims.
- Performance management that requires formal, documented communication.
- Situations involving safety-critical behavior that demands immediate correction.

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: Authors are academic psychologists and consultants; the model relies on conditions of psychological safety that not all workplaces provide.
- Rule conflicts: ['The model may be misused to psychologize performance issues that are structural.', 'Not every behavior is developmental; some require direct management action.']
- Copyright scope: Summarize the immunity-to-change process. Do not reproduce extended case passages.
