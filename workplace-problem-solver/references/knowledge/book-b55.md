## Required user information

- The user's leadership role, decision authority, team size, goals, and current organizational pressures
- The specific behavior, mood, communication pattern, or leadership-style problem and its observable effect
- Feedback from employees, peers, customers, and supervisors rather than the leader's self-assessment alone
- Whether the problem involves temporary stress, chronic overload, conflict, discrimination, harassment, safety, or a health concern
- The team's cultural norms, psychological safety, remote or in-person context, and history with feedback
- Available coaching, 360-degree feedback, development time, organizational support, and accountability
- The work outcomes and relationship indicators that would show improvement

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Executive or senior leader
- Line manager or team leader
- Emerging leader preparing for broader responsibility
- Leadership coach or organizational-development professional
- HR or learning-and-development professional

Power asymmetry:

Leaders influence workload, opportunity, evaluation, pay, and continued employment, so their emotional behavior is not merely interpersonal. Employees may mask fear or disagreement in upward feedback. Application should use confidential multi-source evidence, protect dissent, and avoid asking lower-power employees to regulate a leader's mood or disclose personal emotions without safety and consent.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The framework was developed largely in United States and global executive settings and assumes that emotional expression, coaching, vision, and participative leadership can be discussed openly. Norms for authority, emotional display, privacy, disability, feedback, and collective decision-making differ across cultures and jurisdictions. Employment, discrimination, health, and privacy duties take priority over a leadership-style model.

## Core claims

- Author-framework claim: a leader's emotional tone and behavior shape group climate and can support or impair performance.
- Author-framework claim: resonant leadership draws on self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, and relationship management.
- Author-framework claim: visionary, coaching, affiliative, and democratic styles often create resonance, while pacesetting and commanding styles require selective use.
- Author-development claim: emotional and social competencies can be developed through an intentional process that compares an ideal self with the current self, creates a learning agenda, supports practice, and uses trusting relationships.
- Boundary claim: emotional intelligence is one leadership input and does not replace strategy, technical competence, resources, governance, lawful process, or structural change.

## Decision rules

- If a team's energy, trust, or performance changes with a leader's presence, then examine the leader's observable emotional behavior and its effects using multi-source evidence, unless urgent misconduct requires formal action first.
- If the team needs direction during uncertainty, then use a clear purpose and empathetic visionary style, unless immediate safety requires brief command.
- If development is the goal, then use coaching and practice tied to the person's own goals, unless the issue is deliberate misconduct or an urgent performance failure.
- If a leader relies on pacesetting or commanding behavior, then limit it to conditions that justify it and monitor fear, silence, overload, and dependency.
- If distress, harassment, discrimination, or unsafe workload is present, then address the formal and structural problem rather than reframing it only as emotional resonance.

## Action steps

1. Define the leadership outcome, affected people, time horizon, and current constraints.
2. Collect confidential examples and outcome data from multiple perspectives.
3. Separate the leader's intent from the observed behavior and impact.
4. Identify the current dominant leadership style and one alternative behavior appropriate to the situation.
5. Create a small, reversible practice plan with an observer, coach, or feedback partner.
6. Measure team clarity, voice, trust, workload, quality, and results at a set review date.
7. Correct structural, policy, safety, or conduct issues through the proper organizational channel.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Leader to team, at the start of a change discussion: "The outcome we need is X. I know the uncertainty is affecting people differently, so I want to hear the risks and support you need before we confirm the plan."
- Employee to manager, private feedback when safe: "When the deadline changed without discussion, the team stopped raising risks and rework increased. Could we review priorities and agree on how changes will be communicated?"
- Coach to leader, confidential session: "Your stated intent was urgency. The observed impact was silence and hidden delay. Which behavior could communicate urgency while still inviting critical information?"

## Counterexamples

- A warm and optimistic leader can still make poor strategic, ethical, or technical decisions.
- A quiet or emotionally restrained leader may create trust and clarity without displaying the style expected by the model.
- Commanding behavior can be appropriate during a genuine emergency when roles and duration are clear.
- Positive emotional tone can become forced positivity that suppresses bad news or grief.
- Employee distress may result from understaffing, discrimination, insecurity, or unsafe work rather than a leader's emotional style.

## Contraindications

- Diagnosis or treatment of a mental-health condition using emotional-intelligence concepts
- Use of emotional-style language to excuse harassment, discrimination, retaliation, coercion, or unsafe decisions
- Forced emotional disclosure, unvalidated assessment, or feedback collection without privacy and consent safeguards
- Emergency, legal, clinical, or safety-critical decisions that require qualified protocols
- Use of resonance as a substitute for adequate staffing, role clarity, fair pay, due process, or technical competence

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: The authors have long-standing academic, publishing, coaching, executive-education, assessment, and consulting interests in emotional intelligence and leadership development. Their expertise is substantial, but it creates intellectual and commercial incentives to foreground emotional competencies and leader development over labor power, work design, institutional constraints, and competing leadership models.
- Rule conflicts: ['Emotional-intelligence frameworks face measurement, construct-overlap, and causal-inference debates.', 'A leader-centered account of climate can conflict with research emphasizing systems, team processes, incentives, and working conditions.', 'Style flexibility can conflict with employee expectations for consistency, transparency, and procedural fairness.', 'Positive emotional influence can conflict with authenticity when it becomes emotional labor or impression management.', 'Direct and expressive leadership prescriptions may not transfer across cultures, disabilities, personalities, or remote settings.']
- Copyright scope: Safe use includes bibliographic facts, high-level public concepts, independently written decision rules, and original workplace scripts. Do not reproduce chapters, competency tables, diagrams, cases, assessment items, development exercises, or substantial portions of the book or paid HBR article.
