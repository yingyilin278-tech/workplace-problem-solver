## Required user information

- The audience, decision to be influenced, and action expected after seeing the data
- The source data, definitions, time period, denominator, comparison baseline, and known limitations
- Whether the communication is exploratory analysis, an explanatory presentation, a dashboard, or a stand-alone document
- The single most important message and the evidence that supports or challenges it
- The audience's data literacy, accessibility needs, likely objections, and available attention
- The medium, display size, color constraints, review deadline, and need for an auditable appendix

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Employee presenting results, risks, or recommendations
- Manager reviewing team or performance data
- Analyst, researcher, consultant, or project lead
- HR professional communicating performance or workforce evidence
- Executive preparing a decision presentation

Power asymmetry:

Clear visuals can help a lower-power employee make evidence visible, but the person controlling definitions, access, or the meeting can still suppress context. Preserve source data, disclose limitations, distinguish analysis from advocacy, and avoid using visual emphasis to overstate certainty in high-stakes performance or promotion decisions.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The visual principles are broadly transferable, but color meaning, reading direction, accessibility requirements, privacy, protected-characteristic data, works council consultation, and employment-decision rules vary. Data storytelling does not replace statistical validation, legal review, or local rules governing employee monitoring and automated decisions.

## Core claims

- Publisher-scope claim: effective data communication begins with context, audience, and purpose rather than chart software.
- Book-framework claim: the visual form should match the data and the question being answered.
- Book-framework claim: unnecessary visual elements increase cognitive load and can obscure the message.
- Book-framework claim: size, color, position, contrast, and other preattentive attributes can direct attention and establish hierarchy.
- Book-framework claim: design choices should support usability and meaning, not decoration.
- Book-framework claim: narrative flow can connect evidence to a conclusion or action, while spoken and stand-alone materials may require different treatment.
- Boundary claim: a compelling story must remain faithful to the underlying data and uncertainty.

## Decision rules

- If the audience must make a decision, then define the decision and intended action before choosing a chart.
- If a visual element does not support interpretation, navigation, comparison, or required context, then remove or subordinate it unless accessibility or audit needs require it.
- If one comparison carries the message, then use visual hierarchy and explanatory text to focus attention while retaining enough context to prevent distortion.
- If the chart type makes magnitude or comparison hard to judge, then choose a simpler position- or length-based display rather than decorative or three-dimensional encoding.
- If the evidence is uncertain, incomplete, or observational, then show that uncertainty and avoid a narrative that implies stronger causation.
- If a performance or promotion decision is high stakes, then pair the explanatory view with definitions, source data, and a reviewable appendix.

## Action steps

1. Write an audience statement that identifies who, what they need to know, and what they should do.
2. Validate definitions, sources, filters, denominators, time periods, missing data, and uncertainty.
3. State the intended takeaway in one sentence before building the visual.
4. Choose the simplest visual form that supports the required comparison.
5. Remove clutter and use alignment, white space, contrast, color, and annotation to create a clear hierarchy.
6. Arrange evidence in a logical narrative and distinguish slides for live delivery from stand-alone documents.
7. Test the communication with a representative reader for comprehension, accessibility, and unintended inference.
8. Retain an auditable source or appendix for high-stakes decisions.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Audience: executive; channel: slide title; timing: decision meeting. Wording: "On-time delivery fell after scope increased, while staffing remained unchanged; approve either the reduced scope or two additional roles for the next cycle."
- Audience: performance-review panel; channel: memo with chart and appendix; timing: before calibration. Wording: "The chart shows results against the agreed target using the same period and denominator for all cases. Definitions, exclusions, and source records are in the appendix for review."
- Audience: manager; channel: email with one annotated chart; timing: before a development discussion. Wording: "The strongest improvement is in X; Y remains below the agreed threshold. I recommend one focused action on Y and a review after the next four observations."

## Counterexamples

- A polished story built on incorrect definitions or biased data makes the wrong conclusion more persuasive.
- Aggressive decluttering removes uncertainty, denominators, or comparison context needed for a fair interpretation.
- One highlighted metric hides offsetting harms or invites gaming.
- A live presentation visual is too sparse to function as a stand-alone record after circulation.
- A narrative arc implies causation even though the data only show association.
- Color-only emphasis excludes readers with color-vision differences or fails in grayscale.

## Contraindications

- Use of visual persuasion to conceal uncertainty, adverse evidence, selection bias, or data-quality problems
- Employee monitoring, protected-characteristic analysis, or automated employment decisions without appropriate legal, privacy, and methodological review
- Statistical or causal conclusions that require domain expertise beyond visual communication
- High-stakes decisions where the audience cannot inspect definitions, sources, and contrary evidence
