## Required user information

- The user's specific advancement goal, evidence of readiness, and desired time horizon
- Whether the user needs advice, skill feedback, visibility, access, or active advocacy
- Potential sponsors' authority, influence, incentives, reputation, conflicts, and ability to observe the user's work
- The user's performance record, reliability, distinctive contribution, and ability to create reciprocal professional value
- Organizational promotion criteria, talent processes, network access, and history of favoritism or retaliation
- Identity, cross-gender or cross-cultural dynamics, confidentiality, and boundary risks relevant to the relationship

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Employee seeking advancement
- High-potential or underrepresented professional
- Manager or executive acting as sponsor
- HR or talent leader
- Career adviser distinguishing mentoring from sponsorship

Power asymmetry:

Sponsors risk and use institutional power on behalf of protégés, but they can also control access and create dependency. The protégé may feel pressure to provide loyalty, unpaid labor, personal access, or silence. Sponsorship should be based on observed work, bounded reciprocity, transparent criteria, multiple relationships, and safe routes for reporting conflicts or misconduct.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The book reflects United States corporate advancement and elite professional networks. Sponsorship, self-promotion, senior access, cross-gender relationships, gifts, social contact, and informal advocacy vary across cultures and may be regulated by conflict-of-interest, anti-nepotism, procurement, equality, or public-sector rules.

## Core claims

- Author-framework claim: a mentor typically advises, while a sponsor actively advocates and spends reputational or political capital to create opportunity.
- Author-framework claim: sponsorship is a reciprocal professional alliance rather than a one-way favor.
- Author-framework claim: a protégé should clarify ambition, diagnose readiness, identify plausible sponsors, and demonstrate performance and value.
- Author-framework claim: relying on one sponsor is risky, so a portfolio of support can improve resilience.
- Author-framework claim: women and other underrepresented professionals may face unequal access and distinctive trust or relationship barriers.
- Boundary claim: sponsorship can open doors but cannot substitute for competence, fair selection, or formal safeguards.

## Decision rules

- If the user needs advice or emotional support, then seek mentoring; if the user needs advocacy for a specific opportunity, then assess sponsorship.
- If a potential sponsor cannot observe credible performance, then first create a bounded opportunity to demonstrate value rather than ask for advocacy.
- If the relationship depends on one powerful person, then diversify support and preserve independent evidence of work.
- If the sponsor's request creates an ethical, personal, financial, or reporting conflict, then decline or use a protected channel rather than treating compliance as reciprocity.
- If sponsorship operates outside transparent talent criteria, then pair advocacy with documented evidence and fair process.

## Action steps

1. Define the advancement goal, timing, readiness evidence, and the institutional decision involved.
2. Separate needs for mentoring, coaching, sponsorship, peer support, and formal manager action.
3. Map several possible sponsors by influence, relevance, observation of work, values, and conflict risk.
4. Deliver visible, high-quality work in an appropriate setting and communicate the result without exaggeration.
5. Seek targeted feedback and small advocacy before requesting support for a major decision.
6. Clarify professional boundaries, reciprocity, confidentiality, and the absence of guarantees.
7. Track outcomes, diversify relationships, and disengage if the alliance becomes coercive, conflicted, or ineffective.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Employee to a senior leader after visible work: "My goal is X, and this project produced Y evidence relevant to that role. If you believe I am ready, would you be willing to recommend me for the upcoming opportunity? I would also value any gap you think I should address first."
- Employee to a mentor clarifying the need: "I appreciate your advice. For this next step I also need someone with decision access who has seen my work and can advocate credibly. Who should I build a working relationship with rather than approach cold?"
- Protégé setting a boundary privately: "I value your support, but I cannot take on this request because it creates a conflict with X. I want to keep our relationship professional and focused on the agreed work."

## Counterexamples

- A sponsor may promote a familiar protégé over a stronger candidate, reproducing homophily and favoritism.
- High visibility without strong performance can damage the reputations of both sponsor and protégé.
- A single sponsor's departure can collapse the protégé's access and perceived standing.
- Reciprocity can become coercive when the powerful person expects secrecy, personal loyalty, or uncompensated work.
- Advice to display executive presence can reward narrow cultural norms and disadvantage capable people with different styles.

## Contraindications

- Harassment, coercion, sexual pressure, retaliation, discrimination, or stalking
- Use of sponsorship to bypass lawful, transparent, or collectively agreed hiring and promotion rules
- Undisclosed romantic, familial, financial, procurement, supervisory, or political conflicts
- Requests for confidential information, dishonest advocacy, favoritism, or retaliation against nonparticipants
- Treating a sponsor's support as a guarantee of promotion, pay, or employment

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: Hewlett led a workplace think tank supported by a task force of major companies and sells books and advisory ideas on talent. The framework favors corporate advancement, senior-leader advocacy, and individual relationship strategy and may underweight collective reform, transparent systems, small organizations, and workers who cannot safely access power.
- Rule conflicts: ['Informal advocacy can conflict with equal-opportunity, anti-nepotism, conflict-of-interest, and transparent promotion principles.', 'Strategic self-presentation can conflict with authenticity, cultural norms, disability access, or evidence-based assessment.', 'Reciprocal value can conflict with professional boundaries and the duty of leaders to develop staff fairly.', 'Portfolio sponsorship can conflict with organizational politics or confidentiality across competing leaders.']
- Copyright scope: Safe use includes bibliographic facts, the general mentor-sponsor distinction, publicly listed roadmap themes, and independently written safeguards. Do not reproduce chapters, the complete seven-step map, stories, data tables, exercises, or substantial passages.
