## Required user information

- The agreed role, goal, standard, deadline, and evidence available to both parties
- The exact observable behavior or result, its frequency, context, and impact
- Whether the purpose is recognition, coaching, correction, formal evaluation, discipline, or development
- Prior feedback, resources, training, authority, dependencies, and opportunity to improve
- The recipient's perspective, accessibility or accommodation needs, and possible power or retaliation concerns
- Applicable policy, contract, union process, review deadline, and consequences of the conversation

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Manager giving recognition, coaching, corrective feedback, or development support
- Employee preparing for or responding to a performance conversation
- HR or people operations professional
- Team lead without formal disciplinary authority
- Executive reviewing feedback and performance practices

Power asymmetry:

Manager feedback can affect pay, promotion, reputation, assignments, and employment. Managers should separate development from formal discipline, use standards known in advance, invite correction of the factual record, and avoid using trust language to suppress disagreement. Employees should retain relevant records and use formal review routes when consequences are high.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The guide primarily reflects United States management practice. Expectations for directness, praise, criticism, saving face, documentation, employee representation, privacy, accommodations, and disciplinary procedure differ. The guide cannot replace local law, policy, contract, collective agreement, or professional HR and legal review.

## Core claims

- Publisher-scope claim: regular feedback can help employees meet goals and develop more effectively than relying only on an annual conversation.
- Publisher-scope claim: trust and fair assessment affect whether feedback is received as useful and credible.
- Publisher-scope claim: criticism should emphasize observable performance and improvement rather than personal condemnation.
- Publisher-scope claim: managers should remain calm when a recipient becomes defensive and continue a two-way discussion.
- Publisher-scope claim: strong performance also requires recognition and individualized development rather than attention only to deficits.
- Boundary claim: a developmental conversation is not a substitute for formal process when employment consequences are possible.

## Decision rules

- If feedback concerns a correctable performance gap, then describe the observed behavior, standard, impact, expected next result, available support, and review date.
- If the evidence is incomplete or disputed, then ask for the employee's context and verify the record before assigning motive or escalating consequences.
- If the employee first hears a serious concern at a high-stakes review, then examine whether expectations and prior feedback were adequate before treating the surprise as solely the employee's failure.
- If the recipient becomes defensive, then pause, restate the purpose and evidence, and invite a response without withdrawing a valid standard.
- If performance is strong, then recognize the specific contribution and discuss development rather than offering generic praise.
- If feedback may lead to discipline, pay loss, demotion, or dismissal, then follow formal policy and obtain appropriate HR or legal review instead of relying only on coaching technique.

## Action steps

1. Clarify the purpose, decision authority, standard, evidence, and possible consequence before the conversation.
2. Separate observable behavior and results from personality, intent, hearsay, and labels.
3. Check whether the standard was communicated, feasible, consistently applied, and supported with resources.
4. Open with the purpose and specific evidence, explain the impact, and invite the recipient's account.
5. Agree on the expected result, support, owner, date, and measurement method.
6. Document the agreed plan and unresolved factual differences in proportion to the risk.
7. Follow up at the stated date and update the assessment using new evidence.
8. Use HR, representation, appeal, investigation, or qualified professional support when consequences or allegations are high risk.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Audience: employee; channel: private meeting; timing: soon after a repeated gap. Wording: "The agreed result was X. On these dates, the delivered result was Y, which affected Z. What context or constraint am I missing? Let us agree on the next result, support needed, and review date."
- Audience: defensive feedback recipient; channel: live conversation; timing: after a pause. Wording: "I can see that you disagree with the assessment. I want to separate the factual record from the conclusion. Which example or standard do you believe is inaccurate, and what evidence should we review before deciding the next step?"
- Audience: high performer; channel: one-to-one; timing: soon after the contribution. Wording: "Your work on X changed Y by Z, especially through this specific behavior. Which capability would you like to build next, and what assignment or support would provide a fair test?"
- Audience: manager; channel: follow-up email; timing: after the discussion. Wording: "We agreed that the next outcome is X by date Y, supported by Z, and measured using criterion A. We will review progress on date B; please reply with any correction to this summary."

## Counterexamples

- Frequent feedback remains harmful when it is vague, inconsistent, public, undocumented, or retaliatory.
- A manager frames a predetermined dismissal as an open coaching conversation.
- Behavioral specificity does not cure a biased standard or lack of resources.
- Inviting dialogue becomes false participation when the employee's evidence cannot affect the decision.
- Positive feedback becomes manipulative when praise is used to obtain unbounded extra work.
- An individualized plan creates unequal treatment when comparable cases use different evidence thresholds without justification.

## Contraindications

- Immediate danger, violence, severe harassment, safeguarding issues, or medical crisis
- Formal allegations of discrimination, retaliation, fraud, serious misconduct, or safety violations requiring investigation
- PIP, demotion, pay reduction, dismissal, forced resignation, or an imminent signature deadline without appropriate procedural review
- Use of feedback techniques to diagnose mental health, pressure an admission, bypass representation, or manufacture a record

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: Harvard Business Review Press sells the book and a companion toolkit. The perspective emphasizes manager skill, regular feedback, and individual development and may underweight labor rights, collective processes, structural causes of performance, and the defensive use of feedback documentation.
- Rule conflicts: ['Frequent informal feedback can conflict with union, works council, privacy, accommodation, or formal documentation requirements.', 'A development-first approach can conflict with the need for immediate investigation or safeguarding.', 'Manager discretion and individualized plans can conflict with consistent, auditable treatment.', 'Calm dialogue can conflict with legal advice to limit informal statements during an active dispute.', 'Emphasis on improvement can conflict with recognition that workload, authority, or system design is the primary cause.']
- Copyright scope: Safe use includes bibliographic facts, publisher-stated learning outcomes, high-level paraphrases, and independently written feedback examples. Do not reproduce chapters, checklists, appraisal forms, worksheets, toolkit files, cases, or substantial passages.
