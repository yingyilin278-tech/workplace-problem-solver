## Required user information

- The specific observable behavior or result and when it occurred
- The agreed role, goal, quality standard, deadline, and evidence available to both parties
- The impact on customers, colleagues, risk, cost, learning, or outcomes
- Whether the purpose is recognition, correction, coaching, development, or formal performance management
- The recipient's experience, recent context, likely emotional response, and ability to change the behavior
- The manager's authority, available support, follow-up time, and organizational process
- Whether bias, disability, health, discrimination, misconduct, retaliation, or a protected activity may be involved

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Manager giving feedback to a direct report
- Team lead or project lead without full managerial authority
- Employee giving peer feedback
- Mentor or coach supporting development
- HR professional designing or reviewing feedback practice
- Executive building a feedback climate

Power asymmetry:

Manager feedback can affect pay, assignments, reputation, and continued employment, so a seemingly informal comment may carry coercive weight. Managers should ground corrective feedback in evidence and standards, provide a response opportunity, and distinguish coaching from formal discipline. Peer or upward feedback requires consent, appropriate channels, and protection from retaliation. The recipient's defensiveness should not automatically be treated as proof that the feedback is correct.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The guide reflects general United States management practice. Directness, praise, public recognition, saving face, seniority, and who may correct whom vary across cultures. Local employment law, collective agreements, disability accommodation, protected leave, discrimination rules, and formal discipline procedures govern high-stakes feedback. Feedback should be adapted for language and accessibility needs.

## Core claims

- Publisher-scope claim: feedback should promote useful change, not merely release the giver's frustration.
- Publisher-scope claim: timing and setting affect whether feedback can be heard and used.
- Practice-scope claim: feedback is more actionable when it describes specific behavior and impact rather than personality.
- Publisher-scope claim: an effective discussion is a dialogue that tests understanding and context.
- Publisher-scope claim: both strong and struggling performers need feedback suited to their development needs.
- Publisher-scope claim: feedback should conclude with an action plan and follow-up rather than a vague expectation to improve.
- Boundary claim: a concise feedback conversation cannot replace investigation, accommodation, due process, or a formal performance procedure.

## Decision rules

- If feedback is based on a specific recent behavior and the recipient can act on it, then discuss it promptly in an appropriate private setting.
- If the message concerns praise, then identify the behavior and useful impact rather than offering only a general compliment.
- If the message concerns a gap, then compare observable evidence with an agreed standard and invite relevant context before deciding the remedy.
- If the recipient lacks knowledge or a checking method, then provide instruction or demonstration before relying on open-ended coaching questions.
- If the recipient understands the standard but execution is inconsistent, then agree on practice, support, measures, and a review date.
- If the same issue continues after clear expectations and support, then move to the organization's formal performance process rather than repeating vague feedback.
- If the issue may involve protected conduct, disability, discrimination, harassment, safety, or serious misconduct, then consult the appropriate qualified channel before improvising.

## Action steps

1. Confirm the purpose of the feedback and gather dated, representative evidence.
2. Check the relevant goal, role expectation, standard, and the giver's own possible bias.
3. Choose a private, timely setting and signal the topic so the recipient is not unnecessarily ambushed.
4. Describe the behavior or result and its impact without inferring motive or labeling personality.
5. Ask for the recipient's perspective and listen for missing information, barriers, or a different factual account.
6. Agree on the desired behavior, support, owner, measure, and review date.
7. Ask the recipient to restate the plan to test shared understanding.
8. Document the agreement proportionately and complete the promised follow-up.
9. Escalate through formal processes when repeated failure, serious misconduct, or policy requires it.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Manager to employee, private conversation soon after the event: "In the two most recent client quotes, freight and implementation labor were omitted. That could reduce margin and undermine client trust. Walk me through how you checked the costs. For the next three quotes, use this checklist and attach it to your draft; I will review each one before release."
- Manager recognizing strong work, private or team channel with the employee's comfort considered: "Your risk summary identified the supplier dependency before approval, which let the team negotiate a backup. Please use the same evidence-first structure in next week's executive update."
- Peer to peer, private conversation after asking permission: "Could I share one observation from today's meeting? When the recommendation came before the assumptions, I could not tell which evidence supported it. Adding a one-line assumption statement would help me review it faster."

## Counterexamples

- Immediate feedback can backfire when either person is highly activated or when facts have not been checked.
- A behavior-impact formula can feel manipulative if the stated impact is speculative, exaggerated, or used to conceal a predetermined judgment.
- Frequent feedback can become surveillance when every minor deviation is corrected and the employee has no autonomy.
- Praise can undermine trust when it is generic, public against the recipient's wishes, or distributed inequitably.
- Asking questions is not effective coaching when a novice lacks the underlying mental model and needs direct instruction.
- An action plan will not solve inadequate staffing, conflicting priorities, broken tools, or an impossible target.

## Contraindications

- Use as a substitute for formal investigation, disciplinary policy, accommodation, grievance, or legal review
- Feedback based on rumor, stereotypes, protected characteristics, or unverified personality judgments
- Private confrontation where violence, retaliation, harassment, or evidence destruction is plausible
- Using developmental language to disguise a predetermined adverse employment action
- Sharing confidential performance feedback publicly or with people who lack a legitimate need to know
