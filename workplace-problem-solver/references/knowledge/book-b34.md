## Required user information

- Current workload, working hours, deadlines, meeting load, communication expectations, and recovery time
- Which priorities are formally assigned, who can change them, and what will be delayed if new work is accepted
- The user's nonwork responsibilities, values, health needs, and minimum sustainable boundaries
- The manager's expectations, team norms, emergency definitions, and consequences of saying no
- Whether overload is temporary, chronic, caused by scope, staffing, role ambiguity, perfectionism, or interruptions
- Available leave, flexibility, workload adjustment, occupational-health support, and escalation routes

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Early-career employee
- Individual contributor
- Manager modeling and negotiating sustainable work
- Caregiver or employee balancing multiple life domains
- HR or people leader reviewing work norms

Power asymmetry:

A lower-power employee may not be able to unilaterally block meetings, ignore messages, decline assignments, or protect time without career consequences. Boundary advice should therefore convert personal refusal into visible capacity tradeoffs, ask the authorized decision-maker to choose priorities, and use written confirmation and allies where retaliation risk exists.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The collection is aimed mainly at modern professional work and early-career readers in a United States HBR context. Working-time limits, overtime, leave, caregiving rights, disability accommodation, after-hours contact, and employer health duties vary. Individual boundary-setting may be less transferable in precarious, shift-based, customer-facing, collectivist, or high-power-distance workplaces.

## Core claims

- Publisher-scope claim: hours worked should not be treated as a complete measure of productivity or professional value.
- Collection-scope claim: sustainable balance requires identifying values and priorities, then making time and goals consistent with them.
- Collection-scope claim: boundaries can include calendar availability, meeting limits, communication norms, and explicit limits on extra work.
- Collection-scope claim: breaks, energy management, attention protection, self-care, and physical activity can support functioning.
- Collection-scope claim: warning signs of burnout and deteriorating mental health should prompt protection and support rather than more optimization.
- Boundary claim: personal techniques cannot by themselves fix chronic understaffing, impossible deadlines, coercive availability, bullying, or unlawful work practices.

## Decision rules

- If a new request exceeds available capacity, then state which existing priority will move, shrink, or lose quality and ask the person with authority to choose, unless a genuine emergency temporarily overrides normal planning.
- If interruptions or meetings repeatedly displace critical work, then establish calendar, agenda, channel, and response-time boundaries, unless the role requires immediate availability for defined incidents.
- If work consistently invades recovery time, then test workload reduction, deadline change, staffing, or role redesign before prescribing only better personal habits.
- If a boundary request could trigger retaliation, then use a low-conflict written capacity statement, seek an ally, and preserve records before escalating.
- If distress or sleep, attendance, concentration, or basic functioning is persistently impaired, then seek qualified health support and workplace adjustment rather than treating the book as clinical care.

## Action steps

1. Track one representative workweek, including tasks, meetings, interruptions, after-hours work, and recovery.
2. Rank work by required outcome, decision owner, deadline, effort, and consequence of delay.
3. Define one minimum boundary and one flexible boundary tied to a concrete business need.
4. Offer the decision-maker options to stop, delay, reduce, delegate, or resource conflicting work.
5. Communicate availability, emergency channels, meeting limits, and response expectations in advance.
6. Run a reversible one- or two-week experiment and measure output, quality, delay, energy, and relationship effects.
7. Escalate chronic overload, health deterioration, or retaliation through a manager, HR, occupational health, a union, professional advice, or exit planning.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Employee to manager, written planning message before accepting extra work: "I can take on X. With current capacity, that moves Y to Friday or reduces Z to a draft. Which tradeoff should I use?"
- Employee to colleagues, team channel before a focus block: "I will be offline for focused work from 2:00 to 4:00. For a defined production incident, use the urgent channel; I will respond to other messages after 4:00."
- Employee to manager or HR, private meeting after a repeated pattern: "For the last six weeks, the role has required approximately X hours and regular weekend work. I would like to review scope, deadlines, staffing, and recovery arrangements, then document a sustainable plan for the next two weeks."

## Counterexamples

- Blocking a calendar does not solve a workload whose required hours exceed capacity.
- A hard no can unnecessarily damage trust when a short, rare emergency has a clear end date and recovery plan.
- A flexible boundary may be interpreted as permanent availability when exceptions are not defined.
- More exercise, breaks, or productivity techniques can become an additional burden when chronic organizational overload remains unchanged.
- A boundary that protects one employee can shift hidden work to lower-power colleagues unless workload is redistributed transparently.

## Contraindications

- Acute mental-health crisis, severe functional impairment, self-harm risk, or medical symptoms requiring qualified or emergency support
- Use of self-care or time management to normalize harassment, coercion, unsafe work, illegal hours, or retaliation
- Unilateral refusal in safety-critical or emergency roles without an agreed coverage and escalation plan
- Use of the collection as a substitute for local working-time, leave, disability, wage, or occupational-health law

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: Harvard Business Review Press sells the book and related collections. The volume is designed for accessible early-career self-management and may overemphasize individual agency, professional-office norms, and commercially appealing practical tools while underweighting labor power, frontline constraints, care inequality, and structural workload remedies.
- Rule conflicts: ['Personal boundary advice can conflict with legitimate availability requirements in emergency, clinical, operations, or customer-support roles.', 'Individual productivity advice can conflict with occupational-health evidence that chronic overload requires organizational redesign.', 'Values-based prioritization can conflict with formally assigned duties, collective obligations, or unequal freedom to refuse.', 'Flexible work can improve autonomy but also intensify after-hours work and surveillance.', "Local law, policy, or collective agreements may set stronger or different limits than the book's voluntary practices."]
