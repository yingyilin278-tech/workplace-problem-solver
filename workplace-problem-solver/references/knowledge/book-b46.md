## Required user information

- Why the user wants change and whether the problem is the role, manager, organization, industry, location, workload, or career identity
- The user's skills, evidence of performance, interests, values, constraints, and possible transferable capabilities
- Target roles or professional selves under consideration and how much real-world contact the user has had with them
- Income, savings, benefits, debt, dependents, visa, healthcare, and the minimum acceptable financial runway
- The time available for exploration, learning, networking, and a job search while still employed
- The user's narrative for the change, likely employer objections, and available references or sponsors

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Employee considering an industry or occupation change
- Mid-career professional seeking a new direction
- Worker returning after a break
- Manager supporting an employee's internal move
- Career coach or HR professional

Power asymmetry:

Career changers depend on current employers for income and sometimes references, visas, benefits, or access to projects, while prospective employers control entry standards and may discount unfamiliar experience. Exploration should therefore be discreet where necessary, reversible, and supported by evidence of transferable value. Internal mobility advice also needs protection against retaliation from a manager who may resist losing talent.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The guide primarily reflects United States professional labor markets. Notice periods, noncompete and confidentiality rules, unemployment benefits, healthcare, immigration, credential recognition, age discrimination, and hiring customs vary. Networking, self-promotion, and nonlinear career stories may need adaptation in cultures or sectors that emphasize hierarchy, exams, seniority, or formal credentials.

## Core claims

- Publisher-scope claim: career change can be approached as a normal recurring part of working life rather than as a single irreversible leap.
- Publisher-scope claim: users can explore multiple professional selves instead of waiting for one perfect calling to become obvious.
- Publisher-scope claim: transferable skills and existing experience can be reframed for a new context rather than discarded.
- Publisher-scope claim: low-risk experiments can test a role before the user gives up the current job.
- Publisher-scope claim: financial implications should be modeled and, where possible, behaviorally tested before a lower-paid move.
- Practice-scope claim: a credible transition narrative connects prior choices, present evidence, and future value without pretending the path was perfectly linear.

## Decision rules

- If the user's target remains abstract, then conduct role interviews and a small work sample before enrolling in expensive training or resigning.
- If a target role pays less, then model the full compensation change and test the expected budget for a defined period, unless the current role presents an urgent safety or health risk.
- If the user lacks the target title but has adjacent results, then translate those results into the new employer's problems and evidence requirements before concluding that prior experience is irrelevant.
- If dissatisfaction may be specific to one manager or employer, then test an internal transfer, project, or same-role external search before abandoning the occupation.
- If exploration conflicts with confidentiality, noncompete, visa, or current-job duties, then obtain qualified guidance and redesign the experiment.
- If a change would impose major risk on dependents, then include affected family members in scenario and financial planning before committing.

## Action steps

1. Separate the push factors from the current job and the pull factors toward possible futures.
2. Generate two or three target-role hypotheses rather than one untested destination.
3. Inventory transferable skills using dated results, work samples, stakeholder evidence, and domain knowledge.
4. Interview people doing the work and test assumptions about tasks, entry routes, pay, culture, and progression.
5. Run one reversible experiment, such as a project, course with a real deliverable, volunteer assignment, shadowing opportunity, or freelance sample that complies with current obligations.
6. Model cash flow, benefits, retraining cost, job-search duration, and a downside scenario.
7. Build a truthful career narrative and test it with target-role practitioners before applying at scale.
8. Set a decision date and evidence threshold for continuing, revising, or stopping the transition.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Career explorer to a practitioner, short informational interview request: "I am testing whether my experience in X can transfer to Y. Could I ask you for twenty minutes about the actual work, entry standards, and the mistakes outsiders commonly make? I am looking for reality, not a referral."
- Employee to manager, private development conversation when internal exploration is safe: "I want to test my fit for work involving X without abandoning my current responsibilities. Could we define a time-bounded project with a clear deliverable and review what it shows about my strengths and gaps?"
- Candidate to interviewer, concise transition narrative: "My path moved through A and B, but the consistent thread is solving X. In my current role I produced Y evidence, and I have tested the move through Z. That is the value I would bring to this role."

## Counterexamples

- A side project can create false confidence when it does not reflect the target role's pace, politics, accountability, or full-time economics.
- Living temporarily on a lower budget does not fully test loss of healthcare, pension, status, promotion trajectory, or future earnings.
- Transferable skills do not remove a genuine licensing, credential, language, or technical requirement.
- Networking cannot compensate for weak work evidence in fields that use auditions, portfolios, examinations, or regulated qualifications.
- Staying employed while exploring may be unsafe or impossible when the current role is causing severe health harm or retaliation.

## Contraindications

- Immediate danger, severe health impairment, harassment, discrimination, or retaliation requiring safety, medical, legal, union, or formal organizational support
- Advice that encourages breach of confidentiality, noncompete obligations, fiduciary duties, visa rules, or conflicts of interest
- Irreversible resignation based only on inspiration, a personality test, or a single informational interview
- Use of a general career guide as a substitute for regulated credential, tax, immigration, benefits, or financial advice

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: Harvard Business Review Press sells the guide and related career toolkits. The collection tends to reflect mobile professional and managerial workers with access to networks, discretionary time, education, and savings. It may underweight low-wage work, unemployment, disability, immigration dependence, rural labor markets, unions, and credential barriers.
- Rule conflicts: ['Experiment-first advice can conflict with an urgent need to leave an unsafe or medically harmful role.', 'A coherent career story can conflict with the need to acknowledge discrimination, caregiving, illness, layoffs, or other discontinuities honestly.', 'Networking and self-promotion advice can conflict with cultural norms, organizational policy, or unequal access to influential contacts.', 'Financial prudence can conflict with time-sensitive opportunities, but optimism should not erase downside capacity.', 'Current local rules on noncompetes, confidentiality, benefits, unemployment, and immigration override generic career tactics.']
- Copyright scope: Safe use includes bibliographic facts, publisher-listed benefits, high-level themes, and independently written adaptations. Do not reproduce articles, chapters, worksheets, toolkit files, distinctive exercises, figures, or substantial passages.
