## Required user information

- The type of transition, such as a new company, internal promotion, first management role, turnaround, or international assignment
- The role mandate, success measures, decision rights, resources, reporting line, and time horizon
- The organization's business situation, culture, stakeholders, history, and political landscape
- What made the user successful previously and which assumptions may not transfer
- The manager's expectations, available team capability, early risks, and possible quick wins
- The user's personal capacity, support system, relocation constraints, and consequences of failure

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Employee entering a new role
- Newly promoted manager
- Executive or senior leader
- Internal transfer
- International assignee
- HR, manager, or coach supporting onboarding

Power asymmetry:

A new-role holder is visible but often lacks context, trust, and informal power. Early commitments can create lasting expectations. The user should clarify the mandate and build alliances before making irreversible changes, while recognizing that a manager or board may control the transition's success criteria.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The framework is designed mainly for professional and leadership transitions, with many examples from large organizations. Employment protections, probation, hierarchy, onboarding, public-sector accountability, and cultural expectations differ. The 90-day horizon is a planning device, not a legal probation period or universal performance deadline.

## Core claims

- Publisher-scope claim: transitions create both opportunity and acute vulnerability, and early actions can disproportionately affect later success.
- Author-framework claim: transition strategy should match the situation rather than repeat the approach that worked in the user's previous role.
- Author-framework claim: structured learning, expectation alignment, early wins, alliances, and team assessment can accelerate effective onboarding.
- Author-framework claim: a new leader should diagnose the organization before imposing solutions.
- Boundary claim: transition progress is not measured only by visible activity; premature action can create avoidable resistance or solve the wrong problem.

## Decision rules

- If the user is entering a new role, then clarify mandate, success criteria, decision rights, and stakeholder expectations before promising major outcomes, unless an immediate safety or continuity issue requires action.
- If a prior leadership method succeeded elsewhere, then test it against the new situation, culture, team, and business stage before reusing it.
- If an early win is proposed, then select one that matters to key stakeholders, teaches the user about the system, and does not create hidden long-term costs.
- If the user wants to reorganize or replace people immediately, then first assess capability, context, and system constraints, unless verified misconduct or urgent risk requires prompt formal action.
- If the transition mandate remains contradictory after direct clarification, then document the conflict and negotiate priorities before accepting accountability for incompatible outcomes.

## Action steps

1. Define the transition type, mandate, formal authority, and explicit success measures.
2. Create a learning agenda covering business, culture, people, processes, history, and political relationships.
3. Meet the manager and critical stakeholders to compare expectations, risks, communication preferences, and decision rights.
4. Map supporters, blockers, experts, customers, and cross-functional dependencies.
5. Identify two or three reversible early-win candidates and test their downstream effects.
6. Assess the team and system before making major structural or personnel changes.
7. Review progress at staged checkpoints and renegotiate expectations when evidence changes.

## Ready-to-use wording

- New role holder to manager, first-week meeting: "By day 30, 60, and 90, what outcomes would make you confident that the transition is working? Which decisions are mine, which require consultation, and which require your approval?"
- New manager to team, first team meeting: "I will not assume that what worked in my last role fits here. During the first weeks I will learn how work actually moves, where decisions stall, and what the team believes should be protected or changed."
- New leader to stakeholder, before committing to an early initiative: "This looks like a possible early win. Before I commit, what downstream dependency, historical constraint, or unintended consequence should I understand?"

## Counterexamples

- A visible quick win can damage trust if it shifts costs to another team or reverses a carefully negotiated compromise.
- Extended listening can become avoidance when an urgent operational, safety, or ethical problem requires action.
- A 90-day plan can create false precision in regulated, seasonal, research, public-sector, or long-cycle roles.
- A new leader may misclassify the situation because incumbents provide selective information.
- Replacing a team too quickly can destroy institutional knowledge; waiting too long can preserve serious harm.

## Contraindications

- Use of the framework as a substitute for emergency, safety, compliance, investigation, or crisis procedures
- Use of a 90-day plan as a unilateral employment contract, legal probation rule, or basis for dismissal
- Personnel action without fair process, evidence, and applicable legal or policy review
- Transitions where health, family, immigration, or relocation constraints require qualified support beyond management planning

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: Watkins co-founded a consultancy that sells leadership transition, onboarding, coaching, and organizational services related to the book's domain. The framework emphasizes managerial agency and may underweight labor rights, structural inequality, frontline work, or situations where the mandate itself is unethical or impossible.
- Rule conflicts: ['The emphasis on speed and early wins can conflict with psychological safety, consultation, due process, and deep technical learning.', 'Adapting to organizational culture can conflict with a duty to challenge discriminatory, unsafe, or unlawful norms.', 'Stakeholder alignment can conflict with transparent accountability when powerful stakeholders have incompatible private agendas.', 'Team assessment and restructuring advice can conflict with local employment protections, collective agreements, and procedural fairness.']
- Copyright scope: Safe use includes bibliographic facts, public framework names, high-level transition themes, and original paraphrases. Do not reproduce the free chapter, checklists, self-assessments, diagrams, cases, or substantial portions of the book.
