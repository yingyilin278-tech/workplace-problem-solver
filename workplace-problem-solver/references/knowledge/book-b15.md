## Required user information

- The formal organization chart and the informal sources of influence
- Each stakeholder's goals, dependencies, resources, and decision rights
- The user's objective, reputation, current alliances, and ethical boundaries
- Specific incidents involving conflict, blocked access, credit, assignments, or information
- The culture's tolerance for dissent, networking, self-promotion, and escalation

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Employee
- Manager
- Project lead
- Cross-functional leader
- Executive
- New hire

Power asymmetry:

Political skill matters most when formal authority does not match actual influence. Lower-power employees should map sponsors, gatekeepers, and retaliation risk before confronting a powerful actor. Ethical influence, coalition building, and transparent work records are safer than gossip, covert retaliation, or a public challenge without support.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The book reflects mainstream United States professional workplaces. Informal networking, self-promotion, hierarchy, gift practices, after-hours socializing, and access to senior leaders differ by culture and may disadvantage underrepresented workers. Advice must be adapted to anti-discrimination law, public-sector ethics, union rules, and organizational policy.

## Core claims

- Publisher-scope claim: office politics arise because personalities, agendas, scarce resources, informal rules, and power relationships affect work.
- Publisher-scope claim: political awareness can be practiced without abandoning integrity or becoming a manipulative operator.
- Publisher-scope claim: useful capabilities include gaining influence, handling backstabbers and bullies, managing scarce-resource tension, navigating difficult conversations, and obtaining meaningful assignments.
- Book-adapted claim: before blaming a difficult colleague, test how your own assumptions, history, or behavior may contribute to the conflict.
- Book-adapted claim: share political information with a newcomer only if you would be comfortable with the information becoming public.

## Decision rules

- If a decision repeatedly differs from the formal process, then map informal influencers and pre-decision conversations, unless doing so would require deception or policy violations.
- If the user lacks visibility for completed work, then create routine, factual updates linked to team goals, unless the information is confidential or another person owns the communication.
- If a colleague conflict feels personal, then seek a trusted reality check and test the user's contribution before escalating, unless there is clear abuse, discrimination, or retaliation.
- If resources are scarce, then frame the request around organizational priorities, reciprocal value, and explicit tradeoffs rather than entitlement.
- If sharing political intelligence could become gossip or damage a third party, then share only verifiable, work-relevant process information that could withstand public scrutiny.
- If influence requires deception, favoritism, coercion, or harm to others, then reject that tactic even if it appears effective.

## Action steps

1. Define the work or career outcome and the decision-maker who can authorize it.
2. Map formal authority, informal influence, stakeholder interests, resource control, and likely objections.
3. Assess your contribution to the conflict and obtain one confidential reality check from a trusted person.
4. Build authentic relationships before a crisis by offering useful information, reliability, and reciprocal support.
5. Make achievements and dependencies visible through concise, factual updates.
6. Choose an ethical influence tactic and document agreements, owners, and deadlines.
7. Review whether the political environment remains workable or is imposing unacceptable ethical, health, or career costs.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Manager or sponsor, private career conversation: "I would like to be considered for assignments involving client strategy. Over the last quarter I delivered these results. What evidence or relationships would I need to build before the next staffing decision?"
- Cross-functional resource discussion, meeting or written follow-up: "Both teams have valid priorities. If we fund A first, B moves by two weeks; if B remains fixed, we need one additional owner. Which tradeoff best supports the division goal?"
- New colleague, private orientation conversation: "I can explain the formal decision process and who owns each step. I would rather not characterize people's motives, but I can share the work patterns I have directly observed."

## Counterexamples

- A decision made outside the visible process may reflect confidentiality or emergency authority rather than a hidden political scheme.
- Self-promotion can damage trust when it appropriates team credit or ignores cultural norms.
- Asking the user to examine their own role is inappropriate when clear harassment, discrimination, coercion, or retaliation is occurring.
- Networking advice can reproduce exclusion when access depends on alcohol-centered, after-hours, gendered, or otherwise inaccessible settings.
- Political skill cannot compensate indefinitely for corrupt governance or structurally impossible work.

## Contraindications

- Bribery, fraud, conflicts of interest, retaliation, discrimination, or other conduct requiring formal ethics or legal review
- Immediate safety threats or severe harassment
- Advice that would require gossip, deception, document misuse, or unauthorized disclosure
- Situations where collective bargaining, public-sector rules, or regulated decision procedures control the response

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: Dillon's editorial and professional-manager perspective favors individual navigation, influence, and relationship skill. The publisher has a commercial interest in management guidance. This lens may underweight structural exclusion, labor protections, organizational accountability, and the costs borne by people with less access to informal networks.
- Rule conflicts: ['Political navigation can conflict with transparent process, equal access, and merit-based allocation.', 'Relationship-building advice can conflict with anti-nepotism, procurement, public-sector, or professional ethics rules.', 'Self-reflection can conflict with victim-blaming if used in harassment or discrimination cases.', 'Informal influence strategies may conflict with cultures or systems that require formal written channels.']
- Copyright scope: Safe use includes title and publication metadata, high-level topic coverage, and independently written paraphrases of publicly described practices. Do not reproduce the guide's chapters, checklists, exercises, extended case examples, or paywalled HBR content.
