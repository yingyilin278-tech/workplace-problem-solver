## Required user information

- The leader's formal authority, decision rights, expertise, and accountability for final outcomes
- The team's capabilities, experience, workload, confidence, and access to information
- Observable examples of the leader amplifying or suppressing others' thinking
- The type of work, including whether it is exploratory, routine, regulated, safety-critical, or time-critical
- Who currently generates ideas, frames challenges, debates options, makes decisions, and owns execution
- The consequences of error and whether delegation can be staged, reviewed, or reversed
- Whether team members can disagree safely and whether retaliation, favoritism, or exclusion is plausible

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Manager leading a professional or cross-functional team
- Executive responsible for leadership culture and talent systems
- Project or team lead without full line authority
- HR, learning, or organizational-development professional
- Employee managing upward around a diminishing leader
- Coach supporting a leader's behavior change

Power asymmetry:

A manager controls assignments, visibility, evaluation, and often employment outcomes, so invitations to debate may not feel safe merely because the manager asks. Multiplier practices require explicit dissent protection, fair access to opportunity, and clear decision rights. Lower-power employees should not be made responsible for reforming an abusive or retaliatory leader. In regulated or safety-critical settings, the accountable leader may need to retain or tightly stage decisions.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The framework comes largely from corporate and executive settings and assumes that speaking up, stretching, and delegated ownership are feasible. High-power-distance cultures, unionized workplaces, public agencies, and regulated professions may require more formal authority, consultation, or escalation. Local employment, safety, fiduciary, professional, and data-protection duties override informal leadership practices.

## Core claims

- Publisher-scope claim: some leaders drain intelligence, energy, and capability, while others amplify the intelligence already present around them.
- Author-research claim: the revised edition derives its framework from analysis of more than 150 leaders and identifies learnable disciplines rather than fixed personality types.
- Author-framework claim: Multiplier leadership is expressed through five disciplines commonly labeled Talent Magnet, Liberator, Challenger, Debate Maker, and Investor.
- Author-framework claim: strong leadership does not require being the smartest person in the room; it requires creating conditions in which other people contribute their best thinking.
- Author-framework claim: well-intentioned leaders can accidentally diminish others through behaviors such as rescuing, constant ideation, optimism, or rapid intervention.
- Practice-scope claim: ownership and capability grow when leaders define meaningful challenges, enable rigorous debate, and return responsibility for results to the people doing the work.
- Boundary claim: amplification should not become abandonment, unsafe delegation, forced stretch, or evasion of managerial accountability.

## Decision rules

- If a leader routinely supplies the first and final answer, then ask others for independent thinking before stating a view, unless immediate safety or legal duty requires direction.
- If expertise is underused, then identify each person's strongest capability and match it to a real organizational challenge rather than adding ceremonial participation.
- If team members withhold ideas because the leader dominates or punishes mistakes, then create a defined space for dissent and separate learning errors from negligence or misconduct.
- If the problem is ambiguous, then frame a demanding question and let the team generate options before narrowing the decision.
- If a decision benefits from multiple perspectives, then structure evidence-based debate and make the decision rule and final decision owner explicit.
- If a team member can own an outcome with bounded risk, then transfer ownership, define checkpoints, and resist taking the work back at the first difficulty.
- If delegation would violate law, safety, competence, or fiduciary duty, then retain qualified oversight and use smaller learning assignments.

## Action steps

1. Collect examples of meetings, decisions, and assignments in which the leader either expanded or narrowed others' contribution.
2. Identify the leader's most likely accidental-Diminisher behavior and the conditions that trigger it.
3. Map the team's underused knowledge, judgment, and capabilities.
4. Choose one reversible behavior change, such as speaking last, asking for evidence, or assigning a bounded decision.
5. Frame a real challenge with a clear outcome, constraints, decision owner, and review point.
6. Invite independent views before group discussion and explicitly protect respectful dissent.
7. Run a structured debate that distinguishes facts, assumptions, alternatives, risks, and unresolved questions.
8. Assign ownership for execution and provide resources and checkpoints without reclaiming the work prematurely.
9. Review the result with the team and ask what leader behavior increased or reduced their ability to contribute.
10. Escalate unsafe, retaliatory, discriminatory, or persistently coercive conduct through appropriate channels.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Manager to team, at the start of a decision meeting: "I have a view, but I will share it after each of you gives an independent recommendation and the evidence behind it. Maya owns the final operational proposal; I retain approval because the decision affects regulatory risk."
- Manager to employee, private delegation conversation: "The outcome is a complete client-cost check by Thursday. You own the method and recommendation. Freight, tax, implementation labor, and margin floor are nonnegotiable checks. Bring me the draft at the halfway point; I will not redesign it unless a material risk is missing."
- Employee to a manager who often takes work back, private conversation when safe: "I want to build the judgment to own this work. Could we agree on the decision boundary, two review points, and the conditions that would require you to step in rather than changing the approach during every draft?"

## Counterexamples

- A leader who withholds expertise during a crisis can create delay or harm rather than develop capability.
- Giving ownership without authority, resources, information, or protection is abandonment, not investment.
- Public debate can silence junior, marginalized, or culturally deferential employees even when the leader believes the forum is open.
- Stretch assignments can exploit high performers or create burnout when workload is not reduced elsewhere.
- A leader may appear to invite dissent but still punish disagreement through ratings, access, or future assignments.
- Some routine or regulated tasks require standardization and qualified sign-off more than open-ended challenge.

## Contraindications

- Immediate emergency, safety incident, legal deadline, or regulated decision requiring qualified command
- Delegation beyond a person's competence or authority where errors are difficult to reverse
- Use of stretch language to justify chronic overload, unpaid labor, or denial of support
- Expectation that an employee personally repair an abusive, discriminatory, or retaliatory manager
- Use of debate to relitigate protected rights, established facts, or mandatory policy

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: Wiseman and the Wiseman Group sell Multipliers-branded workshops, assessments, coaching, certification, keynotes, and tools. The framework therefore has commercial incentives and may favor leader behavior change over structural remedies, labor protections, workload design, or power analysis. Corporate success cases may not generalize to frontline or highly regulated work.
- Rule conflicts: ['Speaking last and distributing authority can conflict with emergency command, professional accountability, or a legal duty to direct.', 'Stretching people can conflict with workload limits, disability accommodation, psychological safety, and fair compensation.', 'Rigorous debate can conflict with cultures or contexts where public disagreement carries disproportionate career risk.', 'Strength-based talent deployment can conflict with equal opportunity if leaders repeatedly give visible work to already favored employees.', "Returning ownership can conflict with a manager's obligation to correct known risk or provide adequate training."]
- Copyright scope: Safe use includes bibliographic facts, the high-level Multiplier-versus-Diminisher distinction, names of the five disciplines, general principles, and independently written workplace adaptations. Do not reproduce the chapter, proprietary assessments, diagrams, discussion guides, exercises, case narratives, or branded workshop materials.
