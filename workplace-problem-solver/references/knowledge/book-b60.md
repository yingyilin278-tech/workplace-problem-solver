## Required user information

- The specific conduct, frequency, duration, setting, targets, witnesses, and work impact
- The user's dependence on the person for income, evaluation, assignments, housing, immigration status, healthcare, or reputation
- Whether the problem is incivility, bullying, abusive supervision, discrimination, harassment, retaliation, or danger
- The user's current stress, sleep, functioning, health, support network, and financial exit capacity
- Available avoidance, transfer, reporting, union, legal, occupational-health, and exit options
- The other person's organizational protection, allies, history, and response to boundaries
- Any evidence-preservation, reporting, limitation, or safety deadline

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Employee dealing with a demeaning colleague, manager, client, or customer
- Manager supporting an affected employee
- HR or employee-relations professional
- Contractor or professional dealing with a powerful stakeholder
- Coach or adviser helping a user choose coping, confrontation, or exit

Power asymmetry:

Survival options depend heavily on financial, hierarchical, legal, and social power. A senior professional may be able to avoid or confront a person whom a junior employee cannot. Advice must not blame a target for staying, confronting, or failing to fix the aggressor. Use the lowest-risk effective option and add allies, documentation, and professional support where retaliation is plausible.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The book draws mainly on United States workplace research, stories, and advice. Expectations about direct confrontation, leaving, humor, emotional distance, and authority vary across cultures and jobs. Employment rights, whistleblowing, discrimination, harassment, privacy, working conditions, and constructive dismissal differ by jurisdiction.

## Core claims

- Author-framework claim: the first task is to diagnose the severity, pattern, power structure, and realistic options rather than react automatically.
- Author-practice claim: reducing exposure, creating distance, finding allies, reframing, and protecting one's identity can reduce harm when immediate exit is unavailable.
- Author-practice claim: confrontation and fighting back can work in some settings but require careful assessment of consequences.
- Author-practice claim: leaving can be the healthiest option when the environment remains harmful and alternatives are feasible.
- Author-practice claim: users should also examine whether stress is causing them to spread demeaning behavior.
- Boundary claim: coping strategies do not replace formal protection, clinical care, legal advice, or correction of organizational abuse.

## Decision rules

- If exposure can be reduced without major loss, then start with distance, channel changes, and shorter interactions before confrontation.
- If the person controls important consequences and retaliation is plausible, then build allies, records, and an exit or escalation plan before setting a hard boundary.
- If conduct involves discrimination, harassment, violence, protected reporting, or serious health harm, then use qualified formal channels rather than relying only on coping.
- If psychological reframing reduces distress without denying facts, then use it as a temporary protective tool, unless it delays necessary safety or organizational action.
- If the environment remains harmful after reasonable low-risk steps, then evaluate transfer or exit using financial and health realities rather than treating endurance as virtue.

## Action steps

1. Assess immediate safety, health, functioning, and whether urgent professional support is needed.
2. Document behavior, impact, frequency, witnesses, power, and prior responses.
3. Map avoidance, buffering, ally, reporting, transfer, confrontation, and exit options.
4. Choose one reversible step that minimizes exposure or increases support.
5. Set a boundary or formal request only with a clear goal, channel, evidence, and contingency.
6. Monitor retaliation, health, work outcomes, and whether the behavior changes.
7. Escalate, transfer, or exit when the stop-loss threshold is reached.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Employee to colleague, written boundary when safe: "For this work, please send requests through the project channel and address the task rather than making personal remarks. I will respond there by the agreed deadline."
- Employee to manager or HR, private report: "This is a repeated pattern on these dates, with these work and health impacts. I am requesting reduced exposure, a confidential review, and written confirmation of the next step."
- Employee to manager, transfer request: "The current reporting relationship has not improved after these documented steps. I am requesting a transfer or alternative reporting arrangement by this date while the concern is reviewed."
- User to adviser before exit: "My stop-loss conditions are these health, retaliation, and financial signals. Please help me verify what evidence, notice, benefits, and references I should preserve before acting."

## Counterexamples

- Avoidance can protect the target but leave colleagues exposed or allow the pattern to spread.
- Reframing can become self-gaslighting when it denies real abuse or discrimination.
- A direct boundary can escalate retaliation from a powerful or unstable person.
- Fighting back can make the user resemble the aggressor and weaken evidence or support.
- Leaving may protect health but create financial, immigration, insurance, or career harm if unplanned.

## Contraindications

- Immediate threat, physical violence, stalking, severe harassment, or self-harm risk requiring emergency or qualified support
- Advice to retaliate, threaten, humiliate, sabotage, or unlawfully obtain or disclose information
- Use of coping techniques to normalize discrimination, harassment, coercion, or unsafe work
- Unplanned resignation or irreversible action where essential rights, income, insurance, or immigration status may be affected
- Diagnosis of the other person or substitution of the book for clinical, legal, union, or occupational-health support

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: Sutton's academic expertise and extensive reader correspondence provide broad practical material, but his commercial identity is tied to the provocative label and survival framework. Self-selected stories may overrepresent memorable cases and successful coping, and the individual-survival lens may underweight collective action, formal remedies, and employer duties.
- Rule conflicts: ['Avoidance and reframing can conflict with formal reporting, collective protection, and organizational accountability.', 'Direct confrontation can conflict with trauma-informed practice and retaliation-risk management.', 'Exit advice can conflict with legal, financial, health-insurance, or immigration constraints.', 'Individual coping can conflict with systems approaches that require leaders to remove hazards and redesign incentives.', 'The colloquial framework does not replace legal definitions or professional risk assessment.']
- Copyright scope: Safe use includes bibliographic facts, high-level strategy categories, and independently written decision rules and scripts. Do not reproduce stories, diagnostic tools, exercises, strategy lists in their original form, or substantial passages.
