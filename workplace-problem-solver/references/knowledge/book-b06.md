## Required user information

- Reporting relationship and who controls the disputed decision
- The manager's stated goals, pressures, communication preferences, and decision criteria
- The employee's requested outcome, deadline, and fallback
- Concrete examples, prior conversations, and written records
- Potential retaliation, job-security, or health consequences

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Employees managing relationships with direct or skip-level managers
- Managers who also need to influence executives or other senior stakeholders
- Individual contributors seeking boundaries, visibility, recognition, advancement, or compensation

Power asymmetry:

The framework is designed for influence without formal authority, but it does not remove a manager's control over assignments, ratings, pay, or continued employment. Use lower-risk experiments and written clarification when retaliation is plausible; managing up must not be treated as responsibility for curing abusive leadership.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The examples are oriented toward contemporary professional workplaces, especially United States office norms. Direct advocacy, compensation discussion, and upward feedback may require adaptation in high-power-distance cultures, unionized settings, public-sector systems, or workplaces with formal consultation rules.

## Core claims

- Publisher-scoped claim: workplace influence can be developed through emotional intelligence, relational capital, negotiation, and deliberate conversations even without formal authority.
- Author-scoped claim: ten recurring conversations organize managing-up work, including alignment, styles, ownership, boundaries, feedback, networking, visibility, advancement, money, and quitting.
- Author-scoped claim: understanding a manager's needs and style can improve alignment, while tactful boundary-setting can reduce uncontrolled extra work.
- The detailed mechanics and evidentiary basis of several conversation models were not checked against the full text [uncertain].

## Decision rules

- If work is being judged against unclear priorities, ask the manager to rank outcomes and define success before optimizing effort.
- If a manager adds work beyond available capacity, present explicit tradeoffs and request a priority decision rather than silently absorbing the overload.
- If strong work remains invisible, create a recurring, evidence-based update that connects results to the manager's goals rather than merely listing activity.
- If the behavior involves threats, discrimination, retaliation, or a formal employment action, use policy, evidence, and professional support rather than relying only on relationship tactics.

## Action steps

1. Define the decision or behavior that needs to change.
2. Map the manager's goals, constraints, preferred communication style, and authority.
3. Collect two or three observable examples and identify the smallest reversible request.
4. Choose the relevant conversation type and prepare a concise proposal with options and consequences.
5. Hold the conversation privately, confirm the decision in writing, and test whether behavior changes.
6. Escalate through formal channels or plan an exit if repeated good-faith attempts fail and the cost becomes material.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Audience: Direct manager
- Channel and timing: One-to-one meeting followed by a written recap; Before accepting a new deadline or additional assignment

> I can take this on. With the current workload, that would move Project A from Friday to Tuesday. Which outcome should take priority, or is there another resource we can add?

- Audience: Direct manager
- Channel and timing: One-to-one meeting; At the start of a new project or review cycle

> To make sure I focus on what matters most, what are the top three outcomes you will use to judge success, and how would you like me to keep you updated?

## Counterexamples

- Adapting to a manager's style can become self-erasure when the employee is expected to tolerate humiliation, unlawful conduct, or chronic retaliation.
- A boundary script may fail when the manager refuses to prioritize and still holds the employee accountable for all deadlines; capacity evidence and escalation are then needed.
- Visibility tactics can look political if they claim team results as individual work or bypass agreed reporting lines.

## Contraindications

- Immediate physical danger, serious harassment, discrimination, or threats
- Formal PIP, demotion, pay reduction, termination, or legal dispute requiring jurisdiction-specific review
- Situations where contacting the manager directly would predictably destroy evidence or trigger immediate retaliation
- Severe health impairment that requires medical or professional support rather than another communication experiment

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: The author is an executive coach, speaker, and seller of career-development programs, creating an incentive toward individual communication and influence solutions. Her social-work and research background adds relevant expertise, but structural labor, legal, and organizational-design explanations may receive less weight.
- Rule conflicts: The emphasis on individual influence can conflict with evidence that workload, bias, abusive supervision, compensation systems, or formal authority cannot be repaired by communication alone. Local law, collective agreements, company policy, and psychological-safety research should override scripts when rights or retaliation are involved.
