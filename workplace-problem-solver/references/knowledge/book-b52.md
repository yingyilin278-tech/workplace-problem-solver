## Required user information

- The coachee's role, goal, current challenge, decision authority, and willingness to be coached
- Whether the issue is exploratory, developmental, performance-related, disciplinary, clinical, or legal
- The manager's authority and whether the employee may feel compelled to disclose
- What the employee has already tried, what support is available, and what decision is actually theirs
- The urgency, consequences, confidentiality expectations, and required follow-up

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Manager
- Team lead
- Coach
- Mentor
- HR or learning professional
- Employee using curiosity in peer or upward conversations

Power asymmetry:

A manager's questions are not neutral when the employee's rating, pay, workload, or employment is at stake. Coaching must not conceal evaluation, force personal disclosure, or shift a manager's decision duty onto the employee. State the purpose, limits, and decision owner before using exploratory questions.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The framework is designed for broad workplace use but reflects English-language coaching and leadership norms. Direct questions, silence, self-disclosure, and employee autonomy are interpreted differently across cultures and hierarchies. Coaching does not replace formal performance, accommodation, investigation, or employment procedures.

## Core claims

- Author-framework claim: managers can improve everyday conversations by staying curious longer and slowing the impulse to give advice.
- Author-framework claim: seven recurring questions help open a topic, explore additional context, identify the real challenge, clarify what the person wants, define useful help, expose tradeoffs, and consolidate learning.
- Author-framework claim: short, frequent coaching conversations can become a practical management habit rather than a separate formal event.
- Author-framework claim: asking what help is wanted can reduce rescuing and clarify responsibility.
- Boundary claim: a memorable question sequence is a conversation aid, not a diagnosis, investigation protocol, or substitute for managerial judgment.

## Decision rules

- If the other person owns the problem and has room to decide, then ask exploratory questions before giving advice, unless time-critical safety or compliance action requires clear direction.
- If the first problem statement is broad or emotional, then ask for additional context and the central challenge before choosing a solution.
- If a help request is vague, then clarify what assistance is wanted and what remains the other person's responsibility.
- If a proposed commitment adds work, then identify what must stop, move, or receive more resources before agreeing.
- If the conversation is actually a formal performance, legal, or disciplinary process, then state that purpose explicitly rather than disguising it as coaching.

## Action steps

1. State the purpose, confidentiality limits, available time, and who owns the final decision.
2. Invite the person to name the most important issue in their own words.
3. Ask for additional facts and distinguish the surface request from the central challenge.
4. Clarify the desired outcome and what kind of help would be useful.
5. Explore options and tradeoffs without taking ownership away from the person.
6. Agree on one next action, owner, support, and follow-up date.
7. Ask what was most useful and record any formal commitment separately.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Manager to employee, private developmental one-to-one: "What is the most important challenge you want to work through today? What else should I understand before we look at options?"
- Manager responding to a vague request for help: "What support from me would be most useful, and which part do you want to continue owning?"
- Manager before accepting added work, planning conversation: "If we commit to this, which current priority should stop, move, or receive additional capacity?"

## Counterexamples

- Repeated questioning feels evasive when the manager already has the information and is responsible for making a decision.
- Coaching questions can frustrate a new employee who needs training, standards, or direct instruction.
- An employee may provide the answer they think the manager wants because the power difference remains.
- Exploration can delay urgent action in safety, harassment, compliance, or crisis situations.
- Asking employees to solve chronic understaffing can transfer an organizational problem onto individuals.

## Contraindications

- Emergency, safety, violence, self-harm, or acute clinical situations requiring direct specialist action
- Formal investigation, harassment report, discrimination complaint, or legal dispute requiring a defined protocol
- Use of coaching to avoid giving necessary training, resources, decisions, or clear performance standards
- Questions intended to obtain protected, medical, or private information without a lawful and necessary purpose

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: Bungay Stanier founded companies that sell training, assessments, classes, simulations, bulk books, and enterprise programs based on the framework. The commercial ecosystem favors curiosity-led coaching and may underweight situations requiring expertise, instruction, structural change, or formal authority.
- Rule conflicts: ['Question-led coaching can conflict with the need for direct instruction, timely decisions, and explicit accountability.', "Employee ownership can conflict with the manager's duty to remove structural barriers and provide resources.", 'Short informal coaching can conflict with documentation and due process in performance cases.', 'A standardized question set can conflict with cultural norms, disability access, language needs, or trauma-informed practice.']
- Copyright scope: Safe use includes bibliographic facts, the public existence and functions of the seven questions, and independently written paraphrases. Avoid reproducing the complete proprietary question sequence verbatim, the free chapter, anniversary additions, worksheets, assessments, videos, or substantial passages.
