## Required user information

- The audience, relationship, hierarchy, decision authority, and likely interests
- The exact outcome needed: information, approval, prioritization, behavior change, conflict resolution, or commitment
- The central claim, supporting evidence, tradeoffs, and requested action
- The communication channel, timing, length, confidentiality, and whether a written record is needed
- Known disagreement, cultural or language differences, accessibility needs, and retaliation risk
- What has already been communicated and how the audience responded

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Employee
- Manager
- Executive
- Project or cross-functional lead
- Presenter or proposal owner
- HR or internal communications professional

Power asymmetry:

Persuasion techniques operate differently when the speaker controls assignments, ratings, pay, or employment. A manager must not use credibility, consensus, or common-interest framing to hide coercion or remove meaningful choice. A lower-power employee should combine concise advocacy with evidence, written confirmation, and a safe escalation route when disagreement may trigger retaliation.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The collection reflects mainstream United States business communication. Directness, self-promotion, emotional expression, eye contact, public disagreement, consensus, hierarchy, silence, and decision styles vary across cultures. Communication must also follow local rules for consultation, privacy, accessibility, labor relations, regulated disclosures, and recordkeeping.

## Core claims

- Publisher-scope claim: effective leaders communicate ideas with clarity, credibility, and persuasive impact.
- Publisher-scope claim: successful proposals connect with the audience and adapt to stakeholder decision styles.
- Publisher-scope claim: persuasion is strengthened by framing goals around common interests rather than presenting only the speaker's preference.
- Publisher-scope claim: communication can build consensus and support when the audience understands the idea, evidence, and relevance.
- Boundary claim: a well-delivered message cannot substitute for authority, resources, procedural fairness, factual accuracy, or consent.

## Decision rules

- If the audience must make a decision, then lead with the requested decision, business reason, essential evidence, tradeoffs, and deadline unless confidentiality or due process requires a different sequence.
- If stakeholders have different interests, then identify the shared outcome and adapt the evidence to each legitimate concern without changing the underlying facts.
- If a proposal lacks credibility, then disclose assumptions, limits, risks, and relevant expertise rather than compensating with confidence or status signals.
- If conflict concerns responsibilities or credit, then reconstruct the documented timeline before attempting persuasive reframing.
- If the audience has not consented or lacks safe freedom to disagree, then do not treat apparent agreement as genuine consensus.

## Action steps

1. Define the audience, decision owner, required outcome, and deadline.
2. Write the central message in one sentence and separate facts, interpretation, recommendation, and request.
3. Select the minimum evidence that supports the claim and disclose meaningful uncertainty.
4. Map the audience's likely interests, objections, decision style, and power over the outcome.
5. Choose the channel and structure: conclusion, evidence, impact, options, request, and next step.
6. Invite questions and dissent, then confirm owners, decisions, unresolved issues, and dates in writing.
7. Evaluate the response and revise the message or escalate the underlying resource, authority, or process problem.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Employee to manager in a priority-setting message: "I can complete X by Friday or add Y and move X to Tuesday. The constraint is Z. Please confirm which outcome has priority by this time."
- Cross-functional lead in a decision meeting: "Our shared outcome is X. The evidence supports option A because Y, while the main cost is Z. I need the decision owner to confirm A or B today and name the owner for the next step."
- Employee correcting a credit or responsibility record by email: "I am adding the documented timeline so we can resolve the current decision: on these dates I completed X, received Y, and delivered Z. Please confirm the remaining owner and deadline."

## Counterexamples

- A polished pitch may win approval for a weak idea when assumptions and downside risks are omitted.
- Common-interest framing can become manipulation when stakeholders have genuinely incompatible interests.
- Adapting to a decision style can reinforce bias or hierarchy rather than improve the quality of the decision.
- Consensus-building can delay an urgent decision or blur accountability when a legitimate single owner exists.
- Clear communication cannot fix missing resources, contradictory incentives, or a decision already made in bad faith.

## Contraindications

- Use of persuasion to obtain coerced consent, suppress protected dissent, or conceal material risk
- Harassment, discrimination, retaliation, safety, fraud, or whistleblowing matters requiring formal protected procedures
- Legal, medical, financial, or regulated conclusions that require qualified review
- Confidential investigations where ordinary consensus-building could expose witnesses or compromise evidence
- Communication tactics used in place of resources, authority, due process, or factual verification

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: Harvard Business Review Press sells the collection and related communication products. The anthology favors managerial influence, executive persuasion, and professional-class contexts and may underweight worker voice, collective bargaining, disability access, low-power dissent, and situations where interests cannot be aligned.
- Rule conflicts: ['Persuasion and consensus techniques can conflict with informed consent, independent review, and the right to disagree.', 'Audience adaptation can conflict with consistency, transparency, and equal treatment.', 'Executive-presence norms can conflict with cultural diversity, disability, neurodivergence, gender equity, or language differences.', 'Concise messaging can conflict with complete documentation in disputed or high-risk matters.']
- Copyright scope: Safe use includes bibliographic facts, publisher-listed themes, article titles, and independently written communication rules. Do not reproduce articles, proprietary examples, substantial passages, figures, or exercises.
