## Required user information

- The exact feedback, its source, channel, timing, and whether it concerns appreciation, coaching, or evaluation.
- The user's emotional and identity reaction and any relationship history that changes how the message is heard.
- The applicable goal, standard, decision authority, evidence, and consequence attached to the feedback.
- Whether the user wants to learn, correct an inaccurate record, appeal a decision, improve delivery, or end the exchange.
- Any deadline for response, appeal, signature, or performance action.

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Employees receiving informal comments, performance reviews, or career advice.
- Managers receiving upward feedback or helping others process feedback.
- HR professionals separating coaching, evaluation, and formal process.
- Executives and teams building more useful feedback routines.

Power asymmetry:

Curiosity can improve learning but must not become automatic submission. When feedback controls pay, promotion, discipline, or termination, the recipient should distinguish learning from agreement, request evidence and standards, preserve the record, and use appeal or professional support where needed.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

Feedback norms vary in directness, status sensitivity, face, privacy, and assumptions about authority. The framework does not establish whether an evaluation process is lawful, fair, culturally neutral, accessible, or contractually binding.

## Core claims

- Receiving feedback is difficult because the desire to learn conflicts with the desire to feel accepted and respected.
- A recipient can extract useful information from poorly delivered feedback without endorsing every claim or accepting mistreatment.
- Reactions may arise from disagreement with the content, the relationship with the giver, or a threat to identity.
- Feedback becomes more usable when the recipient clarifies what type of feedback is being offered and what evidence or change it concerns.
- Understanding one's own patterns and impact can improve learning while preserving the right to reject inaccurate or harmful conclusions.

## Decision rules

- If feedback feels wrong, first identify whether the objection concerns truth, the relationship, identity threat, or more than one of these.
- If praise, coaching, and evaluation are mixed, ask the giver to separate the purposes before agreeing to action.
- If the message is vague, request specific behavior, impact, standard, example, and desired next behavior.
- If the feedback affects formal employment rights or contains disputed allegations, listen without making unsupported admissions and respond after reviewing the record.
- If feedback is abusive, discriminatory, coercive, or repeatedly detached from evidence, prioritize boundaries and formal support over becoming more receptive.

## Action steps

1. Pause before responding and write the feedback in neutral, specific terms.
2. Classify the feedback's purpose and the main trigger in the user's reaction.
3. Ask clarifying questions about behavior, impact, standard, examples, and the requested change.
4. Test the message with one trusted, independent source or objective evidence.
5. State what is useful, what remains disputed, and what action the user will or will not take.
6. Confirm formal standards, next review date, and any correction to the written record.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Audience: manager; channel: review meeting followed by email; timing: before accepting a vague rating. Wording: 'I want to use this feedback. Could you separate the specific behavior you observed, the standard it was measured against, its impact, and what result would demonstrate improvement by the next review?'
- Audience: feedback giver; channel: live conversation; timing: when the message feels partly useful and partly inaccurate. Wording: 'I can see the value in the point about response time. I do not yet agree with the broader conclusion about reliability. Can we review the examples and distinguish those two claims?'
- Audience: HR or reviewer; channel: written response; timing: before a formal deadline. Wording: 'I acknowledge receipt of the evaluation. I am reviewing the examples and criteria. I will provide a written response by the stated deadline; receipt should not be read as agreement with disputed facts.'

## Counterexamples

- The feedback contains no examples or standard and is used only to justify a predetermined adverse action.
- The recipient repeatedly asks for more detail to avoid acting on clear, well-supported coaching.
- The giver is harassing or threatening the recipient; curiosity does not make continued direct engagement safe.
- The recipient treats every emotional reaction as proof the feedback is wrong, or treats every useful detail as proof the entire evaluation is right.

## Contraindications

- Immediate danger, severe harassment, acute medical or mental-health crisis, or threats requiring emergency support.
- Definitive legal advice about performance plans, termination, discrimination, wages, signatures, or waivers.
- Any setting where asking for more feedback would expose the user to foreseeable retaliation without a safer channel.
- Use of the framework to compel a target of abuse to accept blame or maintain contact.

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: The authors are Harvard-affiliated educators and commercial consultants through Triad Consulting Group. Their focus on the receiver's agency and learning may underweight biased systems, discriminatory evaluation, organizational politics, and the giver's duty to provide fair evidence and process.
- Rule conflicts: ['Procedural fairness may require correcting the record promptly rather than delaying for extended reflection.', 'A formal evaluation can combine developmental feedback with an employment decision; the purposes cannot always be cleanly separated.', 'Some cultural or neurodiversity contexts require written examples, processing time, an advocate, or explicit communication accommodations.', "A learning posture should not override whistleblower protections, anti-retaliation safeguards, or a union or legal representative's advice."]
