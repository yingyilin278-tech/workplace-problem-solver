## Required user information

- The employee's role, key responsibilities, agreed goals, behavior standards, and review period
- The organization's rating scale, calibration process, compensation linkage, and formal review policy
- Dated examples of results, conduct, coaching, recognition, support, constraints, and prior feedback
- Whether the conversation is developmental, compensation-related, disciplinary, part of a performance improvement plan, or a precursor to termination
- The manager's authority and the employee's access to HR, an appeal route, a union, or another reviewer
- Applicable contract, collective agreement, local employment law, and any response or appeal deadline

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Manager or team leader writing and delivering appraisals
- Employee preparing for or responding to an appraisal
- HR or people operations professional
- Executive overseeing a performance-management process

Power asymmetry:

The book is primarily written for managers, who usually control ratings, documentation, pay recommendations, development opportunities, and escalation. Applying its scripts safely requires advance standards, specific evidence, employee voice, consistent treatment, and a meaningful correction or appeal route. A manager's confidence must not be treated as proof when an appraisal may affect continued employment.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The guidance reflects United States managerial practice and a conventional formal-appraisal system. Rules for notice, employee consultation, access to personnel records, discrimination, unions, works councils, protected leave, disability accommodation, discipline, and dismissal vary by jurisdiction. Direct negative feedback, individual rating, and forced differentiation also require adaptation in high-power-distance and collectivist cultures.

## Core claims

- Publisher-scope claim: effective appraisal is an ongoing performance-management process that includes responsibilities, goals, coaching, recognition, assessment, a face-to-face discussion, and development planning.
- Author-practice claim: managers should define expected results and behaviors before evaluating them.
- Author-practice claim: appraisal quality improves when the manager uses concrete examples and explains how evidence maps to the rating standard.
- Author-practice claim: informal coaching should address weak performance before the formal appraisal whenever reasonably possible.
- Author-practice claim: compensation, development of strong performers, and response to persistent poor performance are connected to appraisal but require distinct decisions.
- Boundary claim: a practical managerial method does not by itself establish that a rating is unbiased, legally defensible, or procedurally fair.

## Decision rules

- If the employee is being rated on a responsibility, goal, or behavior, then confirm that the expectation was communicated and observable during the review period, unless urgent misconduct could not reasonably have been anticipated.
- If a manager identifies a performance gap, then describe the expected result, observed result, dated evidence, impact, and required next outcome before assigning a character label.
- If serious negative feedback first appears in the final appraisal, then pause high-stakes action and examine whether timely coaching, resources, and notice were missing, unless the issue was newly discovered or deliberately concealed.
- If an employee disputes a rating, then allow time to review the document and compare evidence against the stated standard before finalizing, unless a fixed formal deadline requires immediate written preservation of the objection.
- If an appraisal may lead to a performance improvement plan or termination, then follow current policy and qualified local advice rather than relying only on the book's managerial scripts.

## Action steps

1. Identify the decision the appraisal must support and the consequence attached to it.
2. Collect the role description, agreed goals, behavior standards, work products, dated feedback, constraints, and recognition from the full review period.
3. Separate results, observable behaviors, system constraints, and unsupported interpretations.
4. Draft each assessment by linking a standard to evidence and impact.
5. Check consistency against comparable cases and ask HR or a calibration group to review high-stakes ratings.
6. Hold a private two-way discussion, invite missing context, and distinguish disagreement from refusal to improve.
7. Write the next outcomes, resources, owners, dates, measurement method, and appeal or follow-up route.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Manager to employee, private meeting before finalization: "The agreed result was X. The documented result was Y on these dates, which affected Z. What context or constraint should be added before I finalize the assessment?"
- Employee to manager, written follow-up within the review window: "I would like to compare the rating with the stated standard. My evidence is A, B, and C. Please identify the specific evidence supporting the gap and confirm the process and deadline for correcting the record."
- Manager to HR or calibration group, written review before an adverse decision: "Please check whether the same evidence threshold and rating definition have been applied to comparable roles, and whether prior notice, support, and relevant constraints are documented."

## Counterexamples

- A polished appraisal discussion does not repair goals that were vague, changed retroactively, or impossible with the available resources.
- Frequent coaching can become surveillance or harassment when standards are inconsistent or feedback is used selectively.
- An employee's disagreement is not evidence of poor attitude; the employee may possess missing or contradictory records.
- A manager can provide timely support and clear standards yet still face repeated, well-evidenced capability or conduct failure.
- Separating pay from feedback may improve candor in some systems but may be impossible where policy explicitly combines them.

## Contraindications

- Immediate dismissal, forced resignation, discrimination, retaliation, protected leave, disability, wage loss, or legal deadlines requiring qualified local advice
- Use of appraisal language to retroactively create evidence or conceal an already-made termination decision
- Use of subjective ratings without communicated standards, employee response, or a fair evidence review
- Diagnosis of a health or mental-health condition from performance behavior

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: Grote's career and commercial work center on performance-management consulting, training, and books. The book therefore favors formal managerial appraisal and practical execution, may give more weight to manager judgment than employee or labor perspectives, and predates many recent debates about continuous feedback, algorithmic evaluation, remote work, and the abolition of ratings.
- Rule conflicts: ['Manager-led appraisal conflicts with participatory, peer-based, strengths-only, or rating-free performance systems.', "The author's criticism of employee self-rating can conflict with processes that use self-evidence to surface invisible work and power imbalance.", 'Direct rating differentiation can conflict with research and policy concerns about bias, collaboration, and psychological safety.', 'Practical termination guidance can conflict with local due process, collective agreements, protected-status rules, and legal advice.', 'A single annual appraisal can conflict with systems that require continuous goal revision and contemporaneous feedback.']
- Copyright scope: Safe use includes bibliographic facts, high-level process stages, original decision rules, and independently written workplace wording. Do not reproduce chapters, forms, rating tools, sample dialogues, checklists, or substantial passages from the book or paid HBR articles.
