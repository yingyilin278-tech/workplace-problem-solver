## Required user information

- The user's formal role, dependencies, expertise, relationships, resources, and authority in the specific situation
- Who needs whom for what, who controls consequences, and who is accountable for other people's outcomes
- The behavior the user wants to change and whether the challenge is stepping up, stepping back, setting a boundary, or supporting others
- The audience, setting, stakes, cultural norms, and history of dominance, deference, or retaliation
- The user's emotional state, identity exposure, safety, and ability to rehearse or obtain feedback
- Whether power is being used for a role obligation, collective purpose, personal status, coercion, harassment, or exploitation
- Applicable policy, law, professional duty, and formal escalation routes

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Individual contributor seeking appropriate authority or influence
- Manager, executive, or board member using formal power
- Employee interacting with a dominant or controlling leader
- Team member who needs to step up or cede space
- Leadership educator, coach, or organizational adviser

Power asymmetry:

The framework treats power as relational and emphasizes responsibility for people who depend on the role holder. This can help users notice overlooked influence, but it must not erase actual differences in firing authority, money, legal status, identity exposure, or retaliation capacity. Low-power users should not be told that better performance alone can neutralize structural coercion.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The book arises from United States social-psychology research, Stanford business education, executive development, and theatrical practice. Signals of authority, warmth, deference, authenticity, gender, race, disability, age, and class vary across cultures. Employment rights, board duties, retaliation protections, harassment rules, and professional obligations also require jurisdiction-specific verification.

## Core claims

- Publisher-scope claim: power exists in every relationship and is not limited to status, hierarchy, loudness, or commanding presence.
- Author-framework claim: power is relational and depends partly on how much others need a person's role and how responsibly that person handles the dependency.
- Author-framework claim: people can use an actor's mindset to adapt behavior to the role, relationship, setting, and purpose without treating power as a fixed personality trait.
- Author-practice claim: effective power can require stepping forward with authority or stepping back with deference, depending on what the role owes others.
- Research-scope claim: power affects action, attention, perspective taking, helping, objectification, dominance, and other group behavior.
- Boundary claim: role performance does not create legal authority, eliminate structural inequality, or justify manipulation and coercion.

## Decision rules

- If others depend on the user's role for a decision, protection, clarity, or resources, then act with sufficient authority to discharge that duty, unless another person has the legitimate decision right.
- If the user's status behavior is crowding out expertise or voice, then step back, ask, listen, and transfer space to the relevant person rather than performing more dominance.
- If a user feels powerless, then map concrete dependencies, expertise, relationships, and formal routes before concluding that no influence exists, unless immediate danger makes withdrawal or external help safer.
- If a powerful role creates temptation to objectify, intimidate, or prioritize self-image, then add feedback, checks, and accountability before acting.
- If a performance technique conflicts with authenticity, consent, professional duty, policy, or law, then do not use the technique to manufacture compliance.

## Action steps

1. Define the role, purpose, audience, decision, and people affected.
2. Map formal authority, mutual dependencies, expertise, resources, allies, and realistic consequences.
3. Choose whether the role requires stepping up, stepping back, setting a boundary, protecting someone, or asking for help.
4. Rehearse language, posture, pacing, and emotional regulation in a low-risk setting.
5. Act with the minimum force needed for the role and keep the request or decision explicit.
6. Invite feedback from affected people and check for coercion, exclusion, or unintended impact.
7. Document high-stakes decisions and add formal safeguards or escalation where power abuse or retaliation risk exists.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Employee to manager, private planning conversation: "I am responsible for this outcome and have the relevant evidence. I recommend option A for these reasons and need your decision on the remaining resource by this date."
- Manager to team, meeting where the manager has dominated: "I have stated my view, and I do not want my role to end the analysis. I will hold the decision until the people closest to the work identify the risks and alternatives."
- Employee to a controlling leader, private and documented when safe: "I understand the result you need. The current direction creates these specific risks. I propose this alternative and would like the final priority and accountability recorded before execution."
- Leader to an affected employee: "My role gives me authority over this decision and responsibility for its impact. Here is the evidence, the standard, your opportunity to respond, and the review route."

## Counterexamples

- A polished authoritative performance cannot compensate for missing expertise, evidence, legitimate authority, or trust.
- Telling a vulnerable employee that everyone has power can blame them for retaliation or structural exclusion.
- Stepping back can become abdication when a leader must decide, protect, investigate, or allocate resources.
- Stepping up can be read as aggression or punished unevenly because status signals are filtered through gender, race, disability, age, and culture.
- An actor's mindset can become manipulation when the role is used to conceal conflicts, manufacture consent, or evade accountability.

## Contraindications

- Immediate danger, coercive control, assault, stalking, severe harassment, or threats requiring safety and professional support
- Use of performance techniques to impersonate authority, deceive stakeholders, or bypass consent and due process
- Advice that minimizes actual firing power, immigration dependence, economic vulnerability, or retaliation exposure
- Clinical treatment of trauma, anxiety, or another health condition through leadership rehearsal alone
- Substitution of relational-power analysis for law, policy, governance, professional ethics, or qualified advice

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: Gruenfeld is a senior Stanford organizational-behavior scholar who teaches executive programs and a popular course that inspired the book, and she also conducts speaking and consulting. Her actor-and-role lens is supported by relevant research but may favor individual behavioral adaptation, elite professional settings, and leadership development while underweighting labor institutions, material inequality, disability access, and legal remedies.
- Rule conflicts: ['Adaptive role performance can conflict with leadership theories that prioritize stable authenticity and transparency.', 'Individual agency framing can conflict with structural analyses of hierarchy, discrimination, precarity, and collective power.', 'Deference can support collaboration but also reinforce domination or silence expertise when used automatically.', 'Authority signals can conflict with cultural norms and can be interpreted through biased stereotypes.', 'Relational influence cannot override formal governance, professional scope, consent, or legal limits.']
- Copyright scope: Safe use includes bibliographic facts, high-level public concepts, author-attributed research themes, and independently written workplace scripts. Do not reproduce the legal sample, chapters, theatrical exercises, case stories, figures, or substantial passages from the book or course materials.
