## Required user information

- The user's development goal, current career stage, and desired time horizon
- The specific knowledge, feedback, access, visibility, or advocacy the user needs
- Potential mentors' expertise, relationship, authority, conflicts, and available time
- Whether the user needs a mentor, coach, sponsor, peer adviser, teacher, or formal manager decision
- The user's ability to contribute value and maintain a reciprocal professional relationship
- Confidentiality, organizational politics, reporting lines, and retaliation or favoritism risks

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Early-career employee
- Mid-career professional
- Aspiring leader
- Career changer
- Manager or senior colleague acting as mentor
- HR or talent-development partner

Power asymmetry:

Senior advisers and sponsors may influence assignments, ratings, access, and promotion. The mentee may feel unable to refuse requests or disclose discomfort. Mentoring should have clear scope and boundaries, avoid conflicts of interest and favoritism, and never replace formal reporting, investigation, or fair talent processes.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The book reflects United States professional networking and career-advancement norms. Approaching senior people, reciprocal favors, sponsorship, cross-gender mentoring, confidentiality, after-hours contact, gifts, and hierarchy vary by culture, sector, and policy. Users should adapt the approach to local norms and safeguarding requirements.

## Core claims

- Publisher-scope claim: people should actively seek the mentoring needed for a defined development goal rather than wait for a senior person to choose them.
- Publisher-scope claim: clear and realistic goals improve the selection and use of mentoring relationships.
- Publisher-scope claim: mentors, sponsors, and senior advisers can serve different functions in career development.
- Publisher-scope claim: effective developmental relationships are reciprocal, and the mentee should bring preparation and value rather than treat the mentor as an unlimited resource.
- Publisher-scope claim: progress toward professional goals should be evaluated rather than assuming that relationship access equals growth.
- Boundary claim: mentoring can improve information and opportunity but cannot guarantee promotion or correct discriminatory systems.

## Decision rules

- If the user cannot name the developmental need, then define the goal and evidence of progress before approaching a mentor.
- If the need is skill feedback, then seek relevant expertise; if the need is organizational advocacy, then distinguish sponsorship from mentoring and assess the political risk.
- If one person cannot meet all needs, then build a small developmental network rather than expecting a single ideal mentor.
- If a potential mentor has direct evaluative power or a conflict of interest, then define boundaries and consider an additional independent adviser.
- If meetings produce no action or learning, then reset the goal, cadence, preparation, and end point rather than maintaining the relationship for status.

## Action steps

1. Write one development goal, the evidence that would show progress, and the help required.
2. Map possible mentors, sponsors, peers, subject experts, and external advisers by fit and risk.
3. Make a small, specific request with a defined time commitment instead of asking broadly for mentorship.
4. Prepare context, questions, and an update before each conversation.
5. Agree on confidentiality, boundaries, contact method, cadence, and any conflict considerations.
6. Complete agreed actions and report the result concisely.
7. Review progress and either continue, redefine, diversify, or close the relationship respectfully.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Employee to a potential mentor by email: "I am working toward X and value your experience with Y. Would you be open to one 25-minute conversation about these two questions? I will send brief context in advance and follow up with what I apply."
- Mentee at the start of a conversation: "My goal for today is to test this decision, not ask you to make it for me. The evidence I have is X, and the uncertainty is Y."
- Mentee closing or resetting the relationship: "Your advice helped me complete X and learn Y. My next need is Z, which may require different expertise. May I keep you updated occasionally while I broaden my support network?"

## Counterexamples

- A famous or senior mentor may be a poor fit when the person lacks relevant expertise or time.
- Mentoring can become dependency when the mentee seeks permission for every decision.
- A sponsor's advocacy may be perceived as favoritism when selection criteria and access are opaque.
- Reciprocity language can be exploited to demand unpaid labor, loyalty, secrecy, or personal access.
- Informal advice can be wrong, outdated, or shaped by the mentor's own incentives.

## Contraindications

- Harassment, coercion, retaliation, discrimination, or boundary violations requiring a formal protected channel
- Use of mentoring to bypass transparent hiring, promotion, compensation, or performance processes
- Relationships involving undisclosed romantic, financial, supervisory, or procurement conflicts
- Requests to share confidential, proprietary, medical, or legally protected information
- Treating a mentor's opinion as legal, clinical, or financial professional advice

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: Harvard Business Review Press sells the guide and related career collections. The approach favors proactive networking in professional organizations and may understate exclusion from elite networks, unequal access to sponsors, informal favoritism, time poverty, and cultural barriers.
- Rule conflicts: ['Proactive relationship building can conflict with organizational policies on favoritism, procurement, confidentiality, or reporting lines.', 'Sponsorship can improve individual mobility while conflicting with procedural fairness when access is unequal.', 'Reciprocity can conflict with professional boundaries or create hidden obligations.', 'Advice to stand out can conflict with team-credit norms, cultural modesty, or safety in retaliatory environments.']
- Copyright scope: Safe use includes bibliographic facts, publisher-listed mentoring goals, general distinctions among developmental roles, and independently written outreach practices. Do not reproduce articles, chapters, stories, exercises, or substantial passages.
