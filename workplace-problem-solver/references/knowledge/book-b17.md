## Required user information

- The reporting relationship, the manager's decision authority, and who evaluates the user
- The manager's visible priorities, pressures, communication preferences, and expectations
- The user's role, current commitments, recent results, mistakes, and unresolved dependencies
- Specific behavior or words causing difficulty, their frequency, and whether standards are consistent
- The user's goal: alignment, support, feedback, visibility, protection, development, or transfer
- Evidence of retaliation, low ratings, withdrawal of resources, public humiliation, or other adverse action

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Employee reporting to a manager
- New hire or internal transfer learning a manager's expectations
- Individual contributor seeking support or visibility
- Manager managing a relationship with a more senior leader
- HR or coach supporting manager-employee alignment

Power asymmetry:

The manager often controls information, assignments, visibility, ratings, development opportunities, and recommendations. Adaptation to the manager can improve coordination but must not become unquestioning compliance. Lower-power employees should clarify standards, document consequential decisions, and use formal support when retaliation or adverse employment action appears.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The guide reflects concise United States professional-management practice. Norms about hierarchy, directness, dissent, face, after-hours access, and self-promotion vary. It does not establish legal duties or supersede employment law, internal grievance processes, collective agreements, or public-sector rules.

## Core claims

- Publisher-scope claim: the relationship with a manager is a consequential workplace relationship that can be managed deliberately without manipulation or loss of integrity.
- Publisher-scope claim: understanding a manager's priorities and pressures helps an employee frame useful information and requests.
- Publisher-scope claim: setting a constructive tone and managing expectations can improve trust and coordination.
- Publisher-scope claim: trust and respect are earned through reliable delivery, candid communication, and attention to the manager's decision needs.
- Boundary claim: managing up is influence and alignment, not covert politics or surrendering professional judgment.

## Decision rules

- If the manager's request is unclear, then confirm the desired outcome, priority, deadline, and check-in point before committing, unless immediate action is genuinely required.
- If a new request displaces existing work, then explain the consequence and ask the manager to choose the priority rather than silently accepting incompatible commitments.
- If the manager prefers a different communication format, then adapt the channel and level of detail while preserving material facts and risks.
- If feedback is vague or identity-based, then request specific observed behavior, the applicable standard, and evidence of improvement.
- If the user needs development or visibility, then connect the request to the manager's priorities and propose a bounded opportunity with a review date.
- If the manager repeatedly retaliates, fabricates records, humiliates the user, or initiates adverse action, then stop treating the problem as a style mismatch and use documentation and formal safeguards.

## Action steps

1. Map the manager's goals, pressures, decision calendar, preferred channel, and tolerance for detail using observable evidence.
2. Clarify the user's own responsibilities, priorities, decision rights, and nonnegotiable professional obligations.
3. Agree on outcomes, deadlines, quality standards, update frequency, and escalation thresholds.
4. Send short decision-oriented updates that state result, evidence, risk, request, and next step.
5. Raise disagreements privately with options and consequences rather than surprises or personality judgments.
6. Request periodic feedback using concrete examples and document important commitments.
7. Review whether adaptation produces more clarity, support, and consistent standards; escalate if it instead produces greater risk.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Audience: manager; channel: work chat; timing: when a new urgent task arrives. Wording: "I can start this now. Doing so moves Project A's review from Thursday to Friday. Is that the priority you want, or should I send a limited first version of the new task today and keep Project A on schedule?"
- Audience: manager; channel: private one-to-one; timing: before the next evaluation cycle. Wording: "I want to align with how you define strong performance. Which three outcomes matter most this quarter, what evidence will show they are met, and how often would you like an update?"
- Audience: manager; channel: concise email; timing: after a consequential verbal decision. Wording: "To confirm today's decision: I will prioritize X, pause Y until Tuesday, and send the first checkpoint at 3 p.m. tomorrow. Please correct anything I have misunderstood."

## Counterexamples

- The employee perfectly matches the manager's preferred style but still lacks the resources or authority needed to deliver.
- A manager's pressure is not merely a preference when it involves discrimination, harassment, falsification, retaliation, or unsafe work.
- Constantly anticipating an unpredictable manager can increase hidden labor without creating consistent expectations.
- Excessive upward adaptation can suppress necessary dissent, compliance warnings, or technical expertise.
- A detailed status update can be counterproductive when the manager only needs a decision and the evidence can sit in an attachment.

## Contraindications

- Immediate danger, severe harassment, discrimination, retaliation, or safeguarding concerns
- PIP, forced resignation, dismissal, pay reduction, compulsory transfer, or an imminent signature deadline
- Requests to hide risk, falsify status, violate professional duties, or mislead stakeholders
- Use of managing-up advice to blame an employee for a manager's abusive or unlawful conduct
