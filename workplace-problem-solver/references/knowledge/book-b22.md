## Required user information

- The audience, decision owner, stakeholder interests, objections, and existing level of support
- The desired audience action and the present situation the presentation must change
- The central message, supporting evidence, limitations, and strongest counterargument
- The setting, channel, time limit, slide constraints, and whether the document will circulate without narration
- The presenter's authority, credibility, relationship with the audience, and retaliation or reputation risk
- Accessibility, confidentiality, cultural, legal, and approval requirements

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Employee presenting a proposal or result to a manager
- Cross-functional project lead seeking alignment
- Manager or executive communicating strategy or change
- Candidate presenting readiness for a role or promotion
- Consultant, analyst, salesperson, or subject-matter expert

Power asymmetry:

Presentation craft can improve clarity but cannot create authority or safety. A lower-power presenter should identify the real decision maker, pre-wire legitimate concerns when appropriate, retain evidence, and avoid emotional pressure that could be seen as manipulation. In contested employment decisions, presentation technique must not replace a reviewable factual record.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The guide reflects United States business-presentation norms. Expectations for directness, emotional appeal, hierarchy, audience participation, humor, self-promotion, and visual density differ. Accessibility, confidentiality, securities, employment, and public-sector disclosure rules may constrain persuasive choices.

## Core claims

- Publisher-scope claim: persuasion improves when a presentation is designed around audience needs and why the idea matters to them.
- Publisher-scope claim: a coherent narrative helps an audience understand the movement from the current situation to a proposed future.
- Publisher-scope claim: message, visuals, delivery, and tone should work together rather than compete.
- Publisher-scope claim: presenters should prepare for tough audiences and resistance rather than assume information alone will produce agreement.
- Boundary claim: persuasive form should organize truthful evidence, not substitute emotion or design for support.

## Decision rules

- If the audience must decide or act, then state the audience benefit, decision, and next step before designing slides.
- If resistance is predictable, then represent the objection fairly and answer it with evidence, tradeoffs, and implementation detail rather than ignoring it.
- If the presentation is for live delivery, then simplify slides and carry nuance in speech or supporting material; if it must stand alone, then add the context needed for independent reading.
- If one narrative cannot serve stakeholders with materially different authority or concerns, then adapt the framing or hold targeted pre-meetings rather than forcing one generic deck.
- If emotional appeal conflicts with evidence or conceals material risk, then prioritize accuracy and disclosure.
- If the audience cannot authorize the request, then change the requested action to sponsorship, referral, or a decision by the proper owner.

## Action steps

1. Define the audience, decision owner, desired action, objections, and presentation constraints.
2. Write one central message and select only evidence necessary to support or qualify it.
3. Organize the narrative from the audience's current reality through the tension, choice, and credible future state.
4. Create a clear opening, logical sections, meaningful slide titles, and a specific close.
5. Design visuals to reinforce hierarchy and comprehension rather than decorate the message.
6. Rehearse timing, transitions, tone, questions, and answers to the strongest objections.
7. Provide an appendix or follow-up record for detailed evidence, decisions, owners, and dates.
8. After the presentation, confirm the decision and next action in writing.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Audience: executive sponsor; channel: opening statement in a decision meeting. Wording: "Today I am asking for a choice between reducing the launch scope and adding two weeks. I will show the customer impact, delivery risk, and cost of each option, then recommend the lower-risk path."
- Audience: skeptical cross-functional group; channel: live presentation; timing: before proposing the solution. Wording: "Your concern is that this change transfers work to your team without enough capacity. That concern is valid. Here is the measured workload, the two resource options, and the review point that would stop the change if the assumption is wrong."
- Audience: promotion panel; channel: closing slide and spoken request; timing: after presenting role-level evidence. Wording: "The evidence shows sustained work at the next-level scope in these three areas. I am asking the panel to confirm either readiness now or the specific remaining evidence and decision date."

## Counterexamples

- A compelling story oversimplifies a decision whose uncertainty and tradeoffs must remain visible.
- The presenter uses dramatic contrast to manufacture urgency unsupported by evidence.
- A sparse live deck is circulated alone and becomes ambiguous or misleading.
- Audience tailoring turns into telling each stakeholder a materially inconsistent story.
- A polished presentation is aimed at an audience without authority while the actual decision maker is absent.

## Contraindications

- Use of narrative or emotion to conceal material facts, uncertainty, conflicts of interest, or adverse evidence
- Formal investigation, testimony, regulated disclosure, or legal proceeding requiring a prescribed factual format
- Immediate safety or safeguarding issues where escalation is more important than presentation craft
- High-stakes employment decisions that lack reviewable evidence, consistent standards, or due process

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: Duarte cofounded a commercial communication consultancy and sells books, training, and presentation services. The framework favors narrative, audience-centered persuasion, and designed communication and may underweight adversarial evidence review, institutional politics, and situations where formal process matters more than persuasion.
- Rule conflicts: ['Narrative simplicity can conflict with legal, scientific, audit, or due-process requirements for nuance.', 'Audience-centered framing can conflict with the duty to present inconvenient facts consistently across stakeholders.', 'Emotional engagement can conflict with neutral fact-finding or trauma-sensitive communication.', 'Pre-wiring influential stakeholders can conflict with transparent governance if it excludes affected parties.']
- Copyright scope: Safe use includes bibliographic facts, publisher-stated themes, high-level paraphrases, and independently created presentation examples. Do not reproduce chapters, slide makeovers, diagrams, case materials, video content, or substantial passages.
