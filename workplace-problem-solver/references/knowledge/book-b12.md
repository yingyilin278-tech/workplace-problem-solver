## Required user information

- Specific repeated behaviors rather than a global judgment that someone is a jerk
- Who benefits, who is harmed, who witnesses the behavior, and where it occurs
- The person's formal authority, social influence, and relationship with decision-makers
- The user's objective, exposure to retaliation, and ability to change teams or roles
- Existing evidence, organizational norms, and whether the conduct may violate policy or law

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Employee
- Manager
- Team lead
- HR or organizational leader
- Academic or professional team member

Power asymmetry:

The framework explicitly includes harmful supervisors and people who behave differently upward and downward. When the target has less power, a solo accusation can backfire; pattern evidence, corroboration, careful framing around work impact, and a safe escalation channel are more important than direct confrontation.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The examples and workplace systems are primarily United States-oriented. Labels, confrontation styles, reporting expectations, privacy rules, recording laws, and protections against retaliation differ across jurisdictions and cultures. The framework should be translated into local policy and lawful evidence practices.

## Core claims

- Publisher-scope claim: common responses to difficult coworkers often fail because they do not address the motivation and social conditions sustaining the behavior.
- Author-framework claim: seven recurring patterns include the kiss-up/kick-downer, credit stealer, bulldozer, free rider, micromanager, neglectful boss, and gaslighter.
- Author-framework claim: the same person may act differently across audiences, so diagnosis requires observing where, when, and toward whom the behavior occurs.
- Author-framework claim: effective responses often change the social incentives, visibility, timing, or audience around the behavior rather than relying on a single emotional confrontation.
- Boundary claim: colorful labels organize patterns for practical use but do not establish intent, misconduct, or a psychological diagnosis.

## Decision rules

- If a person dominates meetings by speaking first and shaping the frame, then change the meeting sequence and solicit independent input before open debate, unless speed or emergency command requires a single lead.
- If someone behaves well toward leaders but harms lower-power colleagues, then document cross-level pattern evidence and frame escalation around observable impact, unless disclosure would expose sources to serious retaliation without protection.
- If credit is repeatedly reassigned, then make contribution and ownership visible through routine status records before the next milestone, unless confidentiality limits attribution.
- If a free rider benefits from ambiguous teamwork, then define deliverables, owners, and checkpoints, unless the apparent gap is caused by inaccessible resources or an undisclosed accommodation.
- If a manager is neglectful, then make a concrete, time-bounded request for the specific decision or resource needed, unless the manager lacks authority and another decision-maker must be engaged.
- If the conduct suggests coercion, fraud, discrimination, violence, or serious retaliation, then stop using informal typology tactics as the primary response and seek formal or professional support.

## Action steps

1. Record the exact behavior, audience, timing, frequency, and work impact.
2. Map the person's incentives, allies, targets, and situations in which the behavior changes.
3. Test alternative explanations and identify the closest pattern without treating the label as proof.
4. Change one environmental condition, such as speaking order, written ownership, review checkpoints, or the specificity of requests.
5. Track whether the intervention reduces the behavior without increasing retaliation.
6. If escalation is needed, present a concise pattern supported by dates, examples, and business impact rather than attacking personality.
7. Escalate to policy, legal, safety, health, or exit channels when informal action is unsafe or ineffective.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Meeting chair, before discussion: "To avoid anchoring on the first opinion, I would like each function to send its recommendation before we open the floor."
- Manager, written escalation about cross-level behavior: "I am raising a repeated work pattern, not making a character judgment. On these dates, these actions occurred in front of these people and caused these delivery impacts. What process should we use to review it safely?"
- Neglectful manager, message before a deadline: "I need your decision on A or B by 3 p.m. Thursday to keep the launch date. If I do not hear back, which default would you prefer me to use?"

## Counterexamples

- A forceful meeting participant may hold relevant accountability or emergency expertise rather than being a bulldozer.
- Unequal contribution may reflect unclear assignment, hidden work, inaccessible resources, or disability accommodation rather than free riding.
- A manager may appear neglectful because decision rights sit elsewhere or because the user has not made a specific request.
- A factual disagreement or memory difference is not by itself gaslighting.
- Making behavior more visible can provoke retaliation in unsafe hierarchies.

## Contraindications

- Immediate danger, threats, stalking, or violence
- Potential criminal conduct, severe harassment, discrimination, retaliation, or employment action requiring qualified local support
- Use of the typology to diagnose personality disorders or justify harassment
- Evidence collection that requires unauthorized access, deceptive surveillance, or disclosure of protected information

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: West is an academic researcher but also sells books, media work, and speaking services. The memorable taxonomy may encourage overclassification, and a conflict-focused lens may underweight ordinary role ambiguity, resource constraints, or organizational design failures.
- Rule conflicts: ['Motivation-based typologies can conflict with evidence-first approaches that avoid inferring intent.', 'Using social tactics against a difficult coworker can conflict with transparency or procedural fairness norms.', 'Informal countermeasures may conflict with HR, union, regulatory, or legal reporting procedures.', 'Advice calibrated to United States professional workplaces may not transfer directly to high-power-distance or collectivist settings.']
- Copyright scope: Safe use includes bibliographic metadata, the names of the seven publicized patterns, short attributed descriptions, and independently written paraphrases of tactics. Do not reproduce the publisher preview, chapters, case narratives, diagnostic material, or extended passages.
