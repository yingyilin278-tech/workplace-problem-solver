## Required user information

- The reporting relationships, stakeholder incentives, interdependencies, deadlines, and history of the issue
- The manager's stated priorities, communication preferences, pressure points, and actual behavior
- The user's formal role, decision rights, alternatives, credibility, and exposure to retaliation
- The evidence for conflict, obstruction, or incompetence and whether the behavior is repeated or inferred
- The organizational channels available for clarification, escalation, ethics, HR, or cross-functional governance

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Employee working with a manager
- Manager coordinating peers and senior leaders
- Project or product lead without formal authority
- Cross-functional contributor
- Employee seeking visibility, sponsorship, or career support

Power asymmetry:

Managing up occurs under unequal control of evaluation, assignments, information, and advancement. Adapting communication can improve coordination, but it must not become unconditional compliance or responsibility for a manager's misconduct. Users with retaliation risk need written priorities, allies, formal channels, and clear limits on how much interpersonal technique can safely solve.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The guide is oriented toward professional organizations and predominantly United States management norms. Direct disagreement, proactive networking, informal access to senior leaders, documentation practices, and escalation differ across cultures and sectors. Employment law, public-sector rules, union agreements, whistleblower protection, anti-retaliation duties, and formal hierarchy override generic influence tactics.

## Core claims

- Publisher-scope claim: achieving results often requires influence with bosses, peers, and decision makers who are outside the user's formal authority.
- Publisher-scope claim: effective working relationships depend on understanding others' priorities, constraints, styles, and dependencies.
- Publisher-scope claim: networks and coalitions can help move work, obtain sponsorship, and reduce reliance on a single relationship.
- Practice-scope claim: managing up is a reciprocal coordination task, not flattery or silent accommodation of every managerial demand.
- Practice-scope claim: influence is stronger when a request connects the shared objective, evidence, tradeoffs, and a specific decision.
- Boundary claim: interpersonal skill cannot cure serious misconduct, unlawful retaliation, impossible resource constraints, or a fundamentally conflicting mandate.

## Decision rules

- If a new request will displace an agreed priority, then show the tradeoff and ask the person with authority to choose, unless the user already has clear decision rights.
- If a manager's instruction is vague, then confirm the intended outcome, deadline, quality threshold, and decision owner in writing before investing heavily.
- If a cross-functional partner resists, then identify incentives, dependencies, and constraints before escalating personality-based accusations.
- If the user needs support from a senior decision maker, then frame the issue as a shared objective, minimal evidence, risk, requested decision, and next step.
- If adapting to a manager repeatedly requires hiding facts, accepting abuse, or violating policy, then stop treating the issue as a style mismatch and use formal support.
- If one relationship controls all visibility and opportunity, then build a legitimate network before a crisis rather than bypassing the manager covertly.

## Action steps

1. Define the outcome, decision, deadline, and cost of delay.
2. Map who owns authority, expertise, implementation, approval, and informal influence.
3. Identify each critical stakeholder's goals, constraints, likely objections, and preferred communication channel.
4. Prepare a short request using conclusion, evidence, tradeoff, decision, and next step.
5. Offer two feasible options when resources or priorities conflict.
6. Confirm decisions, owners, deliverables, and dates in a neutral written follow-up.
7. Escalate through agreed governance, HR, ethics, or another formal route when normal coordination fails or misconduct appears.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Employee to manager, written message when priorities conflict: "I can complete A by Friday or move capacity to urgent item B and deliver A on Tuesday. If both dates must hold, I need C support. Please confirm which option matches your priority."
- Cross-functional lead to peer, meeting request before escalation: "We share the goal of X. The current dependency is Y, and delay after Thursday affects Z. What constraint am I missing, and can we agree on an owner, deliverable, and checkpoint today?"
- Employee to senior stakeholder, concise update at a decision point: "The conclusion is X. The evidence is A and B. The unresolved risk is C. I need your decision between option one and option two by Thursday; after that I will document owners and proceed."

## Counterexamples

- Adapting a message to a manager does not solve a conflict when the manager's incentives directly oppose the requested outcome.
- More networking can look like bypassing or coalition building against a manager if the purpose and governance are hidden.
- A polished executive summary can omit evidence needed for a dispute, investigation, or high-risk decision.
- Offering options can be inappropriate when law, safety, ethics, or policy permits only one acceptable action.
- A difficult boss may be demanding but fair; one uncomfortable conversation is not evidence of incompetence or abuse.

## Contraindications

- Harassment, discrimination, threats, retaliation, fraud, safety violations, or other matters requiring formal reporting or qualified advice
- Use of influence tactics to manipulate, deceive, conceal material facts, retaliate, or bypass legitimate governance
- Assumption that the lower-power employee is responsible for repairing a manager's abusive or unlawful conduct
- Public confrontation when a private factual clarification or protected formal channel is safer
- Sharing confidential or personal information merely to strengthen a coalition

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: Harvard Business Review Press sells the guide and related management products. The editorial perspective generally prioritizes organizational effectiveness and professional mobility, and may underweight labor rights, unions, structural discrimination, frontline settings, and situations in which influence cannot overcome formal power.
- Rule conflicts: ['Adaptive communication can conflict with transparency if it becomes impression management or concealment.', 'Relationship preservation can conflict with timely reporting of safety, ethics, discrimination, or legal concerns.', 'Informal influence can conflict with procurement, public-sector, compliance, or conflict-of-interest rules.', "Building broad networks can conflict with a manager's expectations, but retaliation risk must be assessed rather than assumed.", 'Advice to meet in the middle can conflict with nonnegotiable rights, professional standards, and formal decision authority.']
- Copyright scope: Safe use includes bibliographic facts, publisher-listed themes, and independently written communication structures. Do not reproduce chapters, article collections, cases, checklists, figures, or substantial passages.
