## Required user information

- The question the visualization must help answer and the action or understanding expected from the audience
- Whether the task is conceptual or data-driven and exploratory or declarative
- The intended audience's data literacy, prior beliefs, decision authority, accessibility needs, and viewing conditions
- The source data, definitions, units, time periods, sample, missing values, uncertainty, and validation status
- The comparison, distribution, relationship, trend, composition, process, or concept that must be shown
- The delivery context, including static report, live presentation, dashboard, mobile screen, print, or executive review
- Any legal, ethical, confidentiality, brand, or regulatory limits on disclosure and persuasion

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Analyst or data scientist communicating findings
- Manager or executive presenting business evidence
- Employee preparing reports, dashboards, or proposals
- Consultant, researcher, journalist, or product leader
- HR or operations professional presenting workforce or performance data
- Designer supporting data-intensive communication

Power asymmetry:

Charts can magnify the authority of the presenter and make design choices appear objective. A lower-power analyst may face pressure to produce a preferred narrative, while leaders may control which data and benchmarks are visible. The creator should preserve source definitions, disclose material uncertainty, and distinguish analysis from a mandated conclusion. Employee-level data require special care because subjects may have little ability to challenge interpretation.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The visual principles transfer broadly, but color meanings, reading direction, numeracy, annotation conventions, accessibility standards, privacy rules, and acceptable employee or customer segmentation vary. Local rules may restrict personal data, protected-class comparisons, financial claims, health information, or forward-looking statements. Color alone should not carry meaning where accessibility is required.

## Core claims

- Publisher-scope claim: visual communication has become a general management skill rather than a specialist-only task.
- Author-framework claim: identifying whether a visualization is conceptual or data-driven and exploratory or declarative helps determine how it should be created and judged.
- Author-process claim: productive chart making begins with talking about purpose, audience, and the idea before choosing a software default.
- Author-process claim: sketching and prototyping make alternative structures cheap to test before polishing.
- Author-method claim: visual perception, hierarchy, position, color, and annotation direct attention and shape interpretation.
- Practice-scope claim: a persuasive chart should make its intended insight easier to see without distorting the underlying evidence.
- Boundary claim: design quality cannot compensate for invalid data, unsupported causality, selective time windows, or omitted uncertainty.

## Decision rules

- If the creator is still searching for patterns, then use exploratory views and preserve complexity, unless confidentiality or data quality prevents analysis.
- If the insight and audience are known, then build a declarative chart with a clear visual hierarchy and annotation rather than presenting every exploratory view.
- If the content is an idea, process, or structure rather than measured values, then use a conceptual diagram instead of forcing it into a quantitative chart.
- If a spreadsheet default does not support the comparison the audience must make, then sketch alternative encodings before formatting.
- If a design choice increases drama but changes apparent magnitude or hides relevant data, then reject it or disclose the limitation.
- If the chart will drive a high-stakes personnel, financial, medical, legal, or public decision, then validate data, definitions, uncertainty, and subgroup effects before optimizing persuasion.
- If the audience cannot explain the chart's main point and evidentiary limit after a short viewing, then revise the hierarchy, labels, context, or chart type.

## Action steps

1. State the audience, decision, main question, and honest evidentiary limit in plain language.
2. Classify the task as conceptual or data-driven and exploratory or declarative.
3. Audit the source, definitions, units, missingness, time frame, denominators, uncertainty, and comparability.
4. Talk through the intended comparison and the audience's likely questions before opening chart software.
5. Sketch several encodings that use position, length, grouping, annotation, and color deliberately.
6. Prototype the strongest alternatives with real data and compare what each makes easy or difficult to perceive.
7. Remove decoration and competing signals that do not serve the question while retaining necessary context.
8. Test comprehension, accessibility, and possible misreadings with representative viewers.
9. Save the data source, transformation notes, and final chart so the claim can be reproduced and challenged.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Analyst to executive, before building a dashboard: "What decision should this view support, and what single comparison must be visible in five seconds? I will preserve the underlying detail in a drill-down rather than place every measure on the first screen."
- Presenter to audience, during a review: "The chart shows an association between response time and renewal rate; it does not establish that response time caused the change. The shaded range reflects the estimate's uncertainty."
- Analyst to a stakeholder requesting a distorted chart, written follow-up: "Starting the axis at 92 would make the difference look much larger than it is. I can highlight the change with a direct label and annotation while retaining a scale that represents the magnitude accurately."

## Counterexamples

- A visually elegant chart can be wrong because the data are incomplete, incomparable, duplicated, or badly defined.
- A declarative headline can turn an uncertain pattern into an unsupported causal claim.
- Simplification can remove the denominator, baseline, variance, or subgroup difference needed for a fair decision.
- A complex exploratory chart may be appropriate for expert diagnosis even though it performs poorly in an executive presentation.
- A familiar chart can outperform a novel design when the audience has limited time or when accessibility and reproducibility are more important than visual distinction.
- Color-coded employee performance charts can expose sensitive information or reinforce bias even when the visual encoding is technically sound.

## Contraindications

- Use as a substitute for statistical, scientific, accounting, legal, privacy, or domain validation
- Visuals that intentionally conceal adverse results, truncate context, misuse area or axes, or imply unsupported causality
- Disclosure of identifiable employee, customer, patient, or protected-class data without authorization and safeguards
- High-stakes automated decisions based solely on a persuasive dashboard or summary chart

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: Berinato and Harvard Business Review Press sell the book, workbook, webinars, and related visualization products. The perspective favors professional knowledge work, business presentations, and persuasion. It may underweight advanced statistical graphics, participatory design, disability access, non-Western conventions, and the organizational incentives that encourage misleading charts.
- Rule conflicts: ['Persuasive visual emphasis can conflict with neutral scientific reporting, audit standards, or balanced disclosure.', 'Simplifying for executives can conflict with the need to expose uncertainty, model assumptions, or subgroup variation.', 'A typology that helps beginners choose a process does not replace domain-specific chart selection or statistical diagnostics.', 'Brand standards can conflict with perceptual clarity or color-accessibility requirements.', 'Narrative sequencing can conflict with interactive exploration when users need to investigate rather than accept a single conclusion.']
- Copyright scope: Safe use includes bibliographic facts, the high-level conceptual-versus-data-driven and exploratory-versus-declarative distinction, general visual principles, and independently created examples. Do not reproduce the book's charts, typology graphics, chapter text, exercises, case vignettes, or workbook pages.
