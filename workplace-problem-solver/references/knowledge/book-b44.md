## Required user information

- The user's target role or direction, current role, career stage, and decision deadline
- Current skills, transferable strengths, evidence of performance, and known gaps
- Feedback from managers, peers, customers, mentors, interviews, or applications
- Available learning time, money, internal opportunities, and external market options
- The costs of staying, transferring, changing fields, or leaving without another role
- Health, financial runway, dependents, immigration, licensing, or other constraints

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Individual contributor
- Manager
- Employee preparing for promotion
- Professional considering a career change
- Learning and development or HR partner

Power asymmetry:

The guide encourages individuals to manage their own development, but access to projects, feedback, sponsorship, training budgets, and promotion remains controlled by managers and organizations. Users should request explicit criteria and create external options without assuming that effort alone will overcome gatekeeping or retaliation.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The guidance reflects mainstream United States professional and managerial work. Promotion systems, credential requirements, mobility, networking, feedback norms, notice obligations, benefits, and career-change risks vary by sector, jurisdiction, and culture. Career planning must incorporate local employment conditions and household constraints.

## Core claims

- Publisher-scope claim: professionals should take active responsibility for assessing and developing the skills needed for their careers.
- Publisher-scope claim: useful feedback, meaningful goals, deliberate learning time, and strengths awareness can convert vague ambition into an action plan.
- Publisher-scope claim: career development includes identifying a next challenge rather than waiting passively for an employer-designed path.
- Publisher-toolkit claim: transferable skills and low-risk role experiments can help people evaluate a move without immediately abandoning current employment.
- Boundary claim: self-directed development improves preparedness but does not guarantee promotion, employment, or access to opportunity.

## Decision rules

- If a career goal is vague, then convert it into a target role, evidence standard, skill gap, experiment, and review date before buying broad training.
- If feedback comes from only one evaluator, then seek additional evidence from work results, peers, customers, mentors, and the external market before concluding that the judgment is definitive.
- If a skill gap is central to the target role, then choose the smallest learning activity that produces observable work evidence, unless the field legally requires a specific credential.
- If a possible career change has not been tested, then use informational interviews, short projects, shadowing, or applications before resigning.
- If the organization offers no credible development opportunity after clear requests and tested performance, then increase external search activity rather than waiting indefinitely.

## Action steps

1. Define one target role or challenge and a decision date.
2. Collect role requirements from internal criteria, job descriptions, and conversations with people doing the work.
3. Inventory current strengths, transferable skills, evidence, and missing capabilities.
4. Request specific feedback tied to the target role and compare it with external market signals.
5. Select one project, course, stretch task, or practice cycle that will create verifiable evidence.
6. Review progress at a fixed date using outcomes rather than hours spent learning.
7. Build internal and external options, then compare staying, transferring, changing, and leaving by cost, reversibility, and likely value.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Employee to manager, private development meeting: "My target is X. Which two or three observable results would demonstrate readiness, and what project could let me produce that evidence by this date?"
- Professional requesting feedback after a completed project: "For the target role I am considering, which behavior should I continue, which gap would most limit me, and what evidence would change your assessment?"
- Career changer to an industry contact by email: "I am testing a move into X and want to understand the work before committing. Could I ask you about the routine responsibilities, entry requirements, and one realistic trial project?"

## Counterexamples

- More learning does not solve a promotion bottleneck caused by headcount, politics, discrimination, or missing sponsorship.
- Playing to strengths can leave a critical weakness unaddressed when the target role requires a minimum standard.
- Stretch assignments can become unpaid role expansion without recognition, authority, or a review commitment.
- Frequent feedback seeking can create noise or dependency when the goal and evidence standard are unclear.
- A career experiment may be misleading when it does not resemble the routine constraints and accountability of the real job.

## Contraindications

- Immediate health or safety danger requiring urgent exit or specialist support
- PIP, termination, discrimination, retaliation, wage, accommodation, or contract disputes requiring formal process or qualified advice
- Irreversible resignation based only on a development plan without financial and market validation
- Use of employer-controlled feedback as the sole measure when bias or conflict of interest is plausible
- Training purchases whose claims, costs, refund terms, and relevance cannot be independently verified

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: Harvard Business Review Press sells the guide, related collections, and paid toolkits. The anthology reflects professional-class, individual-agency, and managerial assumptions and may underrepresent low-wage, unionized, public-sector, precarious, disabled, or highly regulated workers.
- Rule conflicts: ['Personal ownership of career development can conflict with organizational duties to provide fair access, training, accommodation, and transparent promotion processes.', 'Strengths-based development can conflict with competency systems that require specific baseline skills.', 'Continuous learning can become unpaid labor or credential escalation without improved opportunity.', 'Direct feedback and proactive networking norms may conflict with hierarchy, privacy, culture, or retaliation risk.']
- Copyright scope: Safe use includes bibliographic facts, publisher-listed development themes, and independently written planning rules. Do not reproduce articles, chapters, examples, exercises, collection content, toolkit worksheets, or substantial passages.
