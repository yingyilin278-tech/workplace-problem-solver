## Required user information

- The exact words or observable conduct at issue, when and where it occurred, and whether it is isolated or repeated.
- The parties, reporting relationship, decision authority, and any retaliation or employment consequence risk.
- The user's desired outcome: learning, correction, agreement, accountability, relationship preservation, or exit.
- The user's account of impact, intent assumptions, emotions, identity stakes, and contribution to the situation.
- Written records, applicable standards, deadlines, and the earliest irreversible action such as signing or admitting fault.

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Employees raising a concern with a manager.
- Managers giving difficult feedback or correcting performance.
- Peers resolving task, relationship, or attribution conflict.
- HR, mediators, and executives structuring a learning-oriented conversation.

Power asymmetry:

The framework can reduce defensiveness but does not neutralize hierarchy. A lower-power employee should assess retaliation, preserve records, choose a private or written channel, and use a representative or formal process when the other party controls pay, status, evidence, or continued employment.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The conversation model is broadly transferable, but norms for directness, face, apology, emotion, and challenging authority differ. It does not determine legal rights, grievance procedure, evidence admissibility, or local employment obligations.

## Core claims

- A difficult exchange usually contains overlapping disputes about what happened, feelings, and identity.
- Competing interpretations can both contain relevant information; treating intent as known increases blame and defensiveness.
- Beginning from a neutral 'third story' can describe the difference between accounts without requiring either side to concede fault.
- Unacknowledged emotion and identity threat can obstruct listening and problem solving.
- Exploring each party's contribution is more actionable than reducing the event to a single guilty party.

## Decision rules

- If the parties disagree about facts or intent, separate observable events, impact, and inferred motive before discussing responsibility.
- If an opening accusation is likely to trigger defensiveness, begin with a neutral description of the gap between the two accounts and invite the other view.
- If emotion or identity threat is driving the exchange, name the impact carefully and delay final conclusions until arousal is manageable.
- If the user faces retaliation, termination, compelled signing, harassment, or a legal deadline, use the framework only for careful communication unless a formal or professional route is safer.
- If the purpose of the conversation is unclear or the likely benefit does not justify the risk, prepare, document, or use an intermediary rather than confronting immediately.

## Action steps

1. Write two columns: verifiable observations and interpretations.
2. Identify the user's outcome, feelings, identity stakes, and possible contribution without accepting unsupported blame.
3. Draft a neutral third-story opening that states the difference and the reason for discussing it.
4. Ask open questions, paraphrase the other account, and share impact without claiming certainty about intent.
5. Convert learning into a specific request, owner, standard, deadline, and written confirmation.
6. Afterward, record agreed facts, unresolved differences, and the next review point.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Audience: manager; channel: private meeting followed by email; timing: after preparing the facts. Wording: 'We seem to have different understandings of what was agreed and what the deadline required. I would like to compare the timeline, explain the impact on my work, and hear how you saw it so that we can agree on the next step.'
- Audience: peer; channel: direct message or private conversation; timing: before the conflict spreads. Wording: 'I may be missing part of the picture. My record shows that I sent the draft on Tuesday and received the new requirement on Thursday. Can we compare our records and agree on ownership and timing from here?'
- Audience: feedback recipient; channel: live conversation; timing: after a pause if emotions are high. Wording: 'I want to understand the concern. Could you separate the specific behavior you observed, its impact, and what you need me to do differently next time?'

## Counterexamples

- A manager has already issued a document with an imminent signature deadline; an exploratory conversation alone may sacrifice procedural options.
- The other party repeatedly retaliates, threatens, fabricates records, or uses the meeting to compel an admission; neutral framing cannot make the setting safe.
- The dispute concerns a simple, documented calculation error; extensive exploration of identity and feelings may delay a straightforward correction.
- Calling every disagreement a difference in stories can create false equivalence when reliable evidence establishes serious misconduct.

## Contraindications

- Immediate danger, violence, stalking, severe harassment, or a medical or mental-health crisis.
- Requests for a definitive legal conclusion, instructions to sign or waive rights, or advice on evidence collection that may be unlawful.
- Situations requiring emergency reporting, safeguarding, regulatory escalation, or preservation of a formal appeal deadline.
- Use of empathy language to pressure the harmed party into reconciliation or to excuse repeated abuse.

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: The authors are Harvard-affiliated negotiation educators and commercial consultants through Triad Consulting Group. Their perspective favors dialogue, learning, mediation, and relationship-preserving problem solving; this may underweight litigation strategy, union power, enforcement, or situations where the counterpart acts in bad faith.
- Rule conflicts: ['Formal investigations may require a fixed factual statement rather than an exploratory conversation.', 'Anti-harassment, whistleblowing, safety, and labor procedures may require prompt escalation even when relationship repair is desirable.', 'A neutral third story should not erase strong evidence, unequal responsibility, or the need for accountability.', 'Cross-cultural and disability-related communication needs may require an advocate, interpreter, asynchronous channel, or a more direct structure.']
- Copyright scope: Use original paraphrases of concepts and adapted workplace wording. Attribute distinctive concepts such as the third story and the three conversations. Do not reproduce chapters, worksheets, extended examples, or substantial passages; use only brief quotations when legally permitted and necessary.
