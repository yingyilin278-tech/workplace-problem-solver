## Required user information

- The specific observable conduct, frequency, dates, targets, witnesses, and work impact
- Whether the conduct is a one-time lapse, recurring incivility, bullying, abusive supervision, discrimination, harassment, retaliation, or violence
- The user's role and the other person's control over pay, assignments, evaluation, reputation, or employment
- Applicable conduct policies, prior reports, comparable treatment, and available reporting or union channels
- Evidence that the person targets lower-power people or leaves others feeling demeaned, disrespected, de-energized, or oppressed
- The person's performance value, the organizational cost of the behavior, and whether leaders reward or excuse it
- Jurisdiction, confidentiality, safety, and any formal deadline

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Executive or manager setting workplace conduct standards
- HR, people, compliance, or employee-relations professional
- Employee experiencing or observing repeated demeaning conduct
- Team leader protecting team norms
- Organizational consultant or researcher

Power asymmetry:

The framework emphasizes behavior directed at less powerful targets, but lower-power employees may be unable to confront or document misconduct safely. Leaders must carry the burden of investigation and enforcement. Employee-facing advice should use behavior descriptions, allies, confidential channels, and retaliation safeguards rather than encourage unsupported public labeling.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The book is grounded mainly in United States professional workplaces. Workplace civility, directness, profanity, hierarchy, and acceptable conflict vary across cultures. Bullying, harassment, discrimination, protected reporting, privacy, discipline, and dismissal have jurisdiction-specific definitions and procedures that a colloquial label cannot replace.

## Core claims

- Author-framework claim: repeated demeaning behavior is a serious organizational problem, not merely an irritating personality trait.
- Author-framework claim: a useful assessment asks whether targets feel demeaned and whether aggression is directed primarily at less powerful people.
- Author-practice claim: organizations should prevent, identify, and stop destructive conduct even when the person is individually productive.
- Author-practice claim: the total organizational cost includes turnover, reduced effort, distraction, fear, impaired cooperation, and management time.
- Author-practice claim: everyone should examine and restrain their own temporary or recurring harmful behavior.
- Boundary claim: the book's provocative label is not a clinical diagnosis, legal category, or substitute for a fair investigation.

## Decision rules

- If behavior is repeated, demeaning, and concentrated on lower-power targets, then investigate the pattern and organizational cost rather than treating it as a personality clash.
- If the evidence concerns a single bad moment, then address the behavior and impact without assigning a permanent label, unless the event is severe misconduct.
- If a high performer violates conduct standards, then apply the same evidence and consequence process rather than offsetting harm with individual output.
- If an employee reports bullying, discrimination, harassment, retaliation, or violence, then use the appropriate formal process and protections rather than the colloquial rule alone.
- If a leader wants a civilized-workplace rule, then define observable prohibited and expected behaviors, reporting, evidence, response, and consistent consequences.

## Action steps

1. Document specific behavior, context, impact, frequency, witnesses, and power differences.
2. Check immediate safety, retaliation exposure, confidentiality, and formal deadlines.
3. Separate a temporary lapse, a recurring pattern, and legally or professionally defined misconduct.
4. Review policy, prior comparable cases, performance incentives, and organizational cost.
5. Give the accused person a fair opportunity to respond through the proper process.
6. Apply proportionate action, protect affected employees, and correct systems that reward the conduct.
7. Monitor recurrence, retaliation, turnover, team voice, and work outcomes.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Employee to manager or HR, private written report: "On these dates, the following words or actions occurred, usually in front of these people, and had these work effects. I am requesting a confidential review and protection from retaliation."
- Manager to employee, private conduct discussion: "The concern is not a personality label. It is this repeated behavior, its impact, and the stated conduct standard. I want your response before we confirm corrective action."
- Executive to HR, internal review: "Please calculate the full impact, including turnover, absence, rework, complaints, and management time, and apply the same conduct standard regardless of individual revenue or technical performance."

## Counterexamples

- Firm disagreement, direct feedback, or an unpopular decision is not automatically abusive conduct.
- A single regretted outburst may warrant correction without establishing a persistent pattern.
- A socially charming person may still mistreat lower-power employees outside senior leaders' view.
- Removing one harmful person may not fix incentives, workload, or leadership practices that produced the behavior.
- A civility policy can be weaponized against dissent, whistleblowing, cultural difference, disability, or justified anger.

## Contraindications

- Immediate threats, assault, stalking, severe harassment, or self-harm risk requiring safety or emergency support
- Public labeling or humiliation without a fair evidence review
- Use of civility rules to suppress protected reporting, union activity, discrimination complaints, or legitimate disagreement
- Diagnosis of a personality disorder or other condition from workplace behavior
- Discipline or dismissal without current policy, contract, collective-agreement, and legal verification

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: Sutton is an organizational scholar, trade author, speaker, and adviser whose public profile and book sales are strongly associated with the provocative framework. The memorable label increases attention but can encourage oversimplification, moral certainty, and individual blame while underweighting structural causes, cultural difference, rehabilitation, and due process.
- Rule conflicts: ['A strict exclusion rule can conflict with progressive discipline, rehabilitation, restorative practice, and legal due process.', 'Civility norms can conflict with psychological-safety principles when polite conformity suppresses necessary conflict.', 'Behavioral labeling can conflict with trauma-informed, disability-aware, and culturally responsive interpretation.', 'Individual removal can conflict with systems approaches that focus on incentives, workload, and leadership responsibility.', 'Colloquial definitions do not map directly onto legal standards for bullying, harassment, discrimination, or retaliation.']
- Copyright scope: Safe use includes bibliographic facts, high-level public tests, total-cost framing, and independently written procedures and scripts. Do not reproduce the self-test, case narratives, calculations, chapters, or substantial passages.
