## Required user information

- The triggering situation, recurring thoughts and emotions, avoidance behavior, and work impact
- The user's chosen values and the specific action they want those values to guide
- Duration, intensity, sleep, appetite, substance use, panic, self-harm risk, and overall functioning
- Whether the workplace contains genuine danger, harassment, discrimination, retaliation, or material employment risk
- Financial, family, health, immigration, and career constraints relevant to staying or leaving
- Current professional care, diagnosis, medication, treatment plan, and available support only when the user chooses to disclose them

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Employee experiencing rumination, avoidance, or uncertainty
- Professional facing a difficult career choice
- Manager using the framework only for personal reflection
- Qualified mental-health professional using ACT within competence
- Career or workplace adviser maintaining a nonclinical boundary

Power asymmetry:

Acceptance and values language must never be used by managers to encourage employees to tolerate abuse, overload, discrimination, or unsafe work. An employee may practice psychological flexibility while still documenting facts, setting boundaries, seeking formal help, or leaving. Clinical disclosure to an employer should remain voluntary and limited.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The book comes from clinical psychology and an English-language therapeutic tradition. Values, self-observation, emotional disclosure, mindfulness, family obligations, work identity, disability, and help-seeking differ across cultures. Workplace use must respect privacy, accommodation, medical confidentiality, and local professional and employment rules.

## Core claims

- Author-framework claim: psychological flexibility helps people remain open, present, perspective-aware, values-connected, and capable of committed action.
- Author-framework claim: attempts to eliminate or avoid painful internal experiences can narrow life and strengthen struggle.
- Author-framework claim: cognitive defusion changes the relationship to thoughts rather than requiring the person to prove them false.
- Author-framework claim: acceptance means making room for internal experience, not approving harmful external conditions.
- Author-framework claim: values are chosen qualities of action that can orient behavior even when certainty or comfort is unavailable.
- Boundary claim: a self-help presentation of ACT does not diagnose illness, replace individualized treatment, or establish that a workplace is safe.

## Decision rules

- If a distressing thought is being treated as an unquestionable command, then label it as a mental event and check observable evidence before acting.
- If avoidance provides short-term relief but repeatedly moves the person away from chosen values, then test one small values-consistent action unless doing so creates genuine danger.
- If a career decision is driven only by the wish to stop discomfort, then separate internal pain from external facts and compare values, constraints, and reversible options.
- If the environment is actually abusive, discriminatory, or unsafe, then use protection, documentation, formal support, or exit planning rather than framing endurance as acceptance.
- If distress includes self-harm risk, psychosis, severe substance risk, acute panic, or major functional impairment, then seek qualified or emergency care rather than relying on self-guided exercises.

## Action steps

1. Describe the workplace event using observable facts and separate it from thoughts, emotions, urges, and predictions.
2. Notice the internal experience with curiosity and use a brief defusion or present-moment practice.
3. Name the value the user wants to embody in this situation, such as integrity, care, courage, learning, or self-respect.
4. List several small actions that serve the value while preserving safety and options.
5. Choose one reversible action, define the support needed, and set a review time.
6. Observe whether the action expands effective behavior rather than merely suppressing emotion.
7. Escalate to clinical, legal, safety, HR, union, or trusted support when the issue exceeds ordinary self-management.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Private self-reflection before a difficult conversation: "I notice the thought that I will fail if I speak. I do not need to eliminate that thought before taking one prepared, values-consistent step."
- Employee to manager in a private meeting: "This situation is difficult, and I want to act in line with accuracy and respect. The observable issue is X, its impact is Y, and I am requesting Z by this date."
- Employee to a trusted professional when functioning is declining: "My distress has lasted this long and is affecting sleep, concentration, and work in these ways. I need help assessing support and safe next steps rather than trying to manage it alone."

## Counterexamples

- Defusing from a fear thought is unsafe when the fear accurately signals violence, harassment, retaliation, or a serious employment risk.
- Values language can intensify self-blame when the person lacks money, authority, health, or safe choices.
- Acceptance can be misunderstood as passivity toward preventable harm or unfair treatment.
- Mindfulness exercises may increase distress for some people with trauma, panic, dissociation, or other conditions.
- A values-consistent career move can still be financially or practically unsound without market and runway evidence.

## Contraindications

- Immediate self-harm risk, violence, psychosis, severe intoxication, or acute medical or psychiatric crisis
- Use by an unqualified workplace adviser to diagnose, treat, or change medication
- Use of acceptance language to excuse harassment, discrimination, retaliation, unsafe work, or illegal conduct
- Forced mindfulness, emotional disclosure, or values exercises in an employment relationship
- Irreversible resignation based only on relief-seeking without external fact and constraint review

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: Hayes originated ACT and Relational Frame Theory, publishes extensively on them, and sells books, workshops, and training resources. He has a strong theoretical and professional investment in psychological flexibility and may emphasize ACT-consistent interpretations over alternative therapies, structural explanations, or condition-specific treatment.
- Rule conflicts: ['Acceptance-based approaches can conflict with strategies centered on cognitive restructuring, symptom reduction, environmental change, or direct problem solving.', 'Values-guided persistence can conflict with financial, health, caregiving, or safety constraints.', 'Self-as-context and defusion concepts may not fit every culture, cognitive style, disability, or clinical presentation.', 'Broad claims across mental health and performance can exceed what is established for a specific person or workplace problem.']
- Copyright scope: Safe use includes bibliographic facts, public descriptions of psychological flexibility and the six broad ACT processes, and independently written workplace applications. Do not reproduce sample chapters, exercises, worksheets, assessment items, clinical stories, or substantial passages.
