## Required user information

- The concrete disagreement and whether it concerns task, process, relationship, or status
- Each party's goals, interests, and authority
- Whether the user tends to seek or avoid conflict and the counterpart's likely pattern
- The desired outcome and value of the ongoing relationship
- Prior attempts, emotional intensity, evidence, and retaliation risk

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Employees
- Managers
- Team and project leaders
- Executives
- Internal facilitators

Power asymmetry:

The choice to address, defer, involve a third party, or exit depends heavily on hierarchy and retaliation risk. Advice to engage directly should be softened when the counterpart controls employment outcomes or has a pattern of hostility. Managers must separate legitimate authority from status conflict.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The guidance is rooted in professional workplace norms associated with HBR's international but United States-centered audience. Conflict-seeking and conflict-avoiding labels are context dependent; directness, face-saving, hierarchy, and third-party mediation vary across cultures and institutions.

## Core claims

- Publisher-scoped claim: productive conflict work begins by identifying common sources of disagreement and understanding one's own and the counterpart's conflict tendencies.
- Publisher-scoped claim: the response should be selected from options that include direct conversation, third-party help, letting go, and walking away.
- Author-interview claim: conflict can involve relationship, task, process, or status, and different sources may require different responses.
- Author-interview claim: preparation should consider the other person's perspective, the actual disagreement, the user's goal, and the importance of the relationship.
- The precise book taxonomy and ordering of all tools were not checked against the full text [uncertain].

## Decision rules

- If the event is isolated, low-impact, and unlikely to repeat, letting it go may be more useful than confrontation.
- If the conflict affects work, wellbeing, accountability, or a recurring relationship, select a deliberate response rather than habitual avoidance or escalation.
- If both parties seek conflict, lower intensity and narrow the issue; if both avoid it, create a scheduled and structured conversation.
- If the counterpart cannot engage rationally or direct discussion is unsafe, use a trusted third party, formal process, boundary, or exit option.
- Unless a formal duty requires immediate escalation, resolve the issue at the lowest level that has the authority and safety to act.

## Action steps

1. Regulate the immediate stress response before deciding what to do.
2. Identify the type and source of conflict and separate the work issue from personal interpretation.
3. Define the desired outcome and the relationship or wellbeing constraint.
4. Assess both parties' conflict tendencies, authority, and likely reactions.
5. Choose among direct discussion, a third party, a boundary, deliberate non-engagement, or exit.
6. Prepare the opening, conduct the conversation, and record concrete agreements and follow-up.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Audience: Peer or manager
- Channel and timing: Private meeting; After emotions settle but before the pattern repeats

> We both want the launch to succeed, but we appear to disagree about who decides scope changes. I would like to compare our understanding and agree on a decision owner and escalation point for the next change.

- Audience: Conflict-avoidant counterpart
- Channel and timing: Scheduled one-to-one meeting; When indirect hints have not resolved a recurring issue

> I would like to set aside twenty minutes to resolve one specific workflow issue. My goal is not to assign blame; it is to agree on a handoff that prevents another delay.

## Counterexamples

- Treating everyone as a stable conflict seeker or avoider can ignore situational, cultural, trauma, and power effects.
- Direct conversation may worsen a status conflict when the powerful party interprets disagreement as disloyalty.
- Letting go is harmful when the behavior is repeated, discriminatory, unsafe, or materially affects pay, performance, or reputation.
- Third-party involvement can escalate politics when the facilitator lacks neutrality or authority.

## Contraindications

- Immediate danger, threats, or violence
- Harassment, discrimination, fraud, or safety concerns requiring formal reporting
- Formal employment action or legal dispute requiring policy, union, or legal review
- Severe distress where continued engagement is harming health and professional support is needed

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: The author and HBR sell the book, toolkit, and related content. Gallo's consulting and editorial background favors structured, professional conflict engagement, while selected cases may not represent low-wage, highly coercive, or non-United States workplaces.
- Rule conflicts: A style-based conflict model can oversimplify structural power, discrimination, labor rights, trauma, and resource scarcity. Formal reporting duties and local law override advice to keep a conflict private or resolve it at the lowest level.
- Copyright scope: Paraphrase the high-level options, conflict tendencies, and preparation logic. Do not reproduce toolkit worksheets, checklists, sample scripts, or extended passages. Brief attributed quotations may be used only when necessary.
