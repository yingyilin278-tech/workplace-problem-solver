## Required user information

- The triggering event, recurring thought or self-story, emotion, action tendency, and observable behavior
- The user's values, desired identity, career goal, and the decision currently being avoided
- Which facts support the story and which elements are assumptions, inherited rules, or social expectations
- The costs of staying, changing behavior, transferring, or leaving, including health and financial constraints
- The user's authority, organizational support, retaliation risk, and available reversible experiments
- Any severe mental-health symptoms, functional impairment, coercion, harassment, discrimination, or immediate safety concern

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Employee navigating difficult emotions or career uncertainty
- Manager responding to change, conflict, or feedback
- Person considering transfer, role change, or resignation
- Coach or HR professional supporting values-based reflection
- Team leader seeking a less suppressive emotional culture

Power asymmetry:

Values-guided action is constrained by income, hierarchy, immigration status, caregiving, discrimination, and retaliation risk. Emotional agility should create more choice, not pressure a lower-power worker to disclose emotions, tolerate harm, or make an unaffordable exit. Reversible tests, evidence, allies, and protection of options should precede high-risk confrontation or resignation.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The book is written for a broad international audience but is rooted in Western clinical, coaching, and professional-workplace concepts, including Acceptance and Commitment Therapy influences. Values, emotional disclosure, self-construal, hierarchy, and acceptable dissent differ across cultures. Employment, health, disability, privacy, and discrimination issues require current local guidance.

## Core claims

- Publisher-scope claim: emotional agility involves navigating difficult thoughts and feelings with self-acceptance, clear-sightedness, openness, and values-guided action.
- Author-framework claim: suppressing difficult inner experiences and overanalyzing them can both create rigidity.
- Author-framework claim: thoughts and emotions are sources of information but should not automatically direct behavior or be mistaken for facts.
- Author-framework claim: naming patterns and creating distance from thoughts can open a choice between trigger and response.
- Author-framework claim: values provide a direction for small, context-sensitive actions rather than a promise of comfortable feelings.
- Boundary claim: acceptance of an internal experience does not require acceptance of harmful external conditions.

## Decision rules

- If a recurring thought is treated as a fact or identity, then relabel it as a thought, emotion, or story before making a career decision.
- If the person is bottling an emotion or brooding on it, then acknowledge and name the experience with curiosity before choosing a values-consistent action.
- If a career choice is driven only by immediate relief or social expectation, then identify the relevant values and run a reversible experiment before an irreversible move, unless health or safety requires prompt exit.
- If values conflict, then specify the concrete behavior each value supports, the cost of each option, and the time horizon rather than declaring one value authentic.
- If emotional distress is severe, persistent, or functionally impairing, then use qualified clinical support and do not present emotional agility as treatment.

## Action steps

1. Describe the triggering situation and distinguish the event from the story attached to it.
2. Name the thought and emotion precisely using language such as "I am noticing the thought that...".
3. Allow the experience without suppressing it, obeying it, or forcing positivity.
4. Identify two or three values relevant to the decision and define observable behaviors that express them.
5. Generate low-conflict, balanced, and strong-boundary options and compare their cost, reversibility, and risk.
6. Choose one small values-consistent experiment with a date and success signal.
7. Review what changed in the environment, behavior, and functioning, then continue, escalate, seek support, or exit.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Private reflection before a career decision: "I am noticing the story that leaving means failure. The feeling is fear, not a command. The values at stake are health, responsibility, and growth. What small action honors them without closing my options?"
- Employee to manager, private meeting after reflection: "I want to contribute and also work sustainably. The current pattern is X, its impact is Y, and I would like to test Z for two weeks before we decide on a larger change."
- Employee to a trusted adviser, before resigning: "Please help me separate facts, emotions, and inherited expectations. I want to compare staying, transferring, and leaving against my values, health threshold, financial runway, and reversible next steps."

## Counterexamples

- Accepting an emotion can be misused to encourage acceptance of bullying, discrimination, retaliation, or unsafe work.
- Values language can rationalize overwork when achievement crowds out health, care, or fairness.
- A small experiment is inappropriate when immediate danger, acute health deterioration, or a fixed legal deadline requires prompt action.
- Creating distance from thoughts can become intellectualization that avoids necessary grief, anger, evidence gathering, or confrontation.
- An employee may be emotionally agile and still lack the authority, resources, or labor-market options to change the situation.

## Contraindications

- Acute self-harm risk, psychosis, severe functional impairment, or another crisis requiring immediate qualified support
- Use of the framework to diagnose, treat, or replace psychotherapy or medical care
- Use of acceptance or values language to normalize harassment, discrimination, coercion, unsafe work, or unlawful practices
- Irreversible resignation, disclosure, confrontation, or legal action without assessing safety, evidence, deadlines, and financial consequences
- Employer-mandated emotional disclosure or assessment that compromises privacy or informed consent

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: David developed and commercially teaches the Emotional Agility framework through books, speaking, advising, assessments, learning products, and certification. Her clinical and research training supports the synthesis, but the branded model may emphasize individual agency and selected supportive evidence while underweighting structural labor constraints and construct overlap with established psychological flexibility approaches.
- Rule conflicts: ['Acceptance-based practices can be misunderstood as conflicting with direct problem solving, collective action, or urgent exit from harm.', 'Values-guided choice can conflict with financial necessity, formal duties, family obligations, and unequal power.', 'The framework overlaps with Acceptance and Commitment Therapy and broader psychological flexibility research, so claims unique to the Emotional Agility brand require caution.', 'Encouraging emotional openness can conflict with workplace privacy, stigma, discrimination, or retaliation risk.', 'Small values-based changes can conflict with situations where organizational redesign or legal protection is the necessary remedy.']
- Copyright scope: Safe use includes bibliographic facts, the public four-part framework names, high-level concepts, and original workplace adaptations. Do not reproduce the book, free chapter, quiz, certification materials, exercises, stories, paid HBR article, or substantial passages.
