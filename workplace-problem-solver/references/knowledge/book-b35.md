## Required user information

- The duration and intensity of exhaustion, cynicism or detachment, and reduced work efficacy
- Workload, control, rewards, community, fairness, and value conflicts in the current job
- Work hours, recovery time, staffing, role clarity, support, and recent organizational changes
- The user's physical and mental-health symptoms, functional impairment, and immediate safety concerns
- Available workplace adjustments, leave, medical care, employee assistance, union support, and management authority
- Whether the user wants symptom relief, work redesign, escalation, leave, or exit planning

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Employee experiencing chronic work strain
- Manager reviewing team workload and work design
- HR or occupational health professional
- Executive responsible for organizational systems
- Researcher or practitioner assessing workplace conditions

Power asymmetry:

Employees may be unable to change workload, control, rewards, fairness, or values without managerial action. Advice must not shift organizational responsibility onto the lower-power worker. When speaking up could affect employment, the user needs safe evidence, allies, formal adjustment routes, and an exit threshold rather than only self-care.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The book and much of the underlying research come from North American and professional-service contexts, although burnout research is international. Work-hour rules, medical leave, disability accommodation, occupational health duties, unions, and cultural norms about overwork differ. The framework is not a legal standard and must be combined with current local law and care pathways.

## Core claims

- Publisher-scope claim: burnout should not be treated solely as an individual's failure to cope because workplace conditions can be central causes.
- Author-framework claim: chronic mismatches in workload, control, reward, community, fairness, and values can increase burnout risk.
- Research-scope claim: burnout is commonly discussed through exhaustion, cynicism or depersonalization, and reduced professional efficacy.
- Author-framework claim: durable prevention requires improving the relationship between people and work, not only teaching individuals to tolerate harmful conditions.
- Boundary claim: burnout is an occupational phenomenon and is not, by itself, a medical diagnosis.

## Decision rules

- If distress is concentrated around chronic workload, low control, poor reward, damaged community, unfairness, or value conflict, then assess work redesign and organizational remedies before prescribing only personal resilience, unless immediate health needs require clinical care first.
- If symptoms are persistent, severe, generalized beyond work, or impair basic functioning, then recommend qualified medical or mental-health assessment rather than labeling the condition as burnout.
- If a manager sees the same exhaustion pattern across multiple employees, then investigate shared work conditions before attributing the problem to individual weakness.
- If the employee cannot safely change the work mismatch, then prioritize documentation, formal support, leave, transfer, or exit planning, unless a safe and credible organizational remedy is available.
- If advice would require working harder to recover from overwork, then reject it unless the added activity clearly reduces demands or restores recovery.

## Action steps

1. Check immediate safety, severe impairment, and whether professional health support is needed.
2. Map the problem across workload, control, reward, community, fairness, and values using concrete examples.
3. Identify which mismatches the user can change, influence, or only protect against.
4. Choose one reversible work intervention, such as reprioritization, staffing, decision authority, recovery time, conflict repair, or clearer recognition.
5. Make the request with evidence, a business impact, a feasible option, and a review date.
6. Track symptoms and work conditions rather than relying on a single good or bad day.
7. Escalate to occupational health, medical care, HR, a union, leave, transfer, or exit planning when risk or impairment persists.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Employee to manager, private planning meeting before the next workload cycle: "The current workload requires approximately X hours against Y available hours, and these three priorities conflict. Please choose what should stop, move, or receive additional capacity. I would like to review the effect in two weeks."
- Manager to team, workload review after repeated strain signals: "I am treating this as a work-design issue, not an individual resilience problem. We will review workload, decision control, recognition, team friction, fairness, and value conflicts, then assign owners for changes."
- Employee to occupational health or HR, confidential channel when functioning is declining: "The symptoms have persisted for this period and are affecting these functions. I need information about confidential assessment, leave, adjustment, and protection from retaliation."

## Counterexamples

- A short period of fatigue after an unusual deadline is not sufficient to establish chronic burnout.
- Reducing workload may not solve distress driven primarily by bullying, discrimination, moral injury, illness, or conflict outside work.
- More autonomy can increase strain when responsibility rises without resources or clarity.
- Team wellness activities can worsen cynicism if impossible workload and unfair systems remain unchanged.
- An employee may need urgent clinical care even when organizational conditions contributed to the problem.

## Contraindications

- Suicidal thoughts, self-harm risk, psychosis, inability to perform basic functions, or other acute health emergencies
- Use of the framework to diagnose depression, anxiety, trauma, or any medical condition
- Advice that delays medical care, emergency support, protected leave, or legally required accommodation
- Use of burnout language to normalize harassment, unlawful hours, retaliation, or unsafe work

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: Maslach co-developed the proprietary Maslach Burnout Inventory, and both authors have professional interests in burnout research, assessment, consulting, and workplace interventions. Their organizational mismatch model may receive more emphasis than competing models or debates about overlap with depression.
- Rule conflicts: ['The occupational burnout framing can conflict with research arguing that severe burnout symptoms substantially overlap with depression.', "Organizational redesign recommendations can conflict with the user's limited authority or the employer's immediate operational constraints.", "Individual recovery practices can be useful but conflict with the book's message when presented as the sole remedy.", "Local occupational-health, disability, and leave law may define duties differently from the book's practical framework."]
- Copyright scope: Safe use includes bibliographic facts, high-level descriptions of the six areas of worklife and burnout dimensions, and original paraphrases linked to public research. Do not reproduce chapters, the proprietary Maslach Burnout Inventory, scoring materials, figures, or substantial passages.
