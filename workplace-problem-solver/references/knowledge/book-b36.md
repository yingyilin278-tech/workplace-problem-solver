## Required user information

- The duration, frequency, severity, and work linkage of depressive, anxiety, exhaustion, cognitive, sleep, and somatic symptoms
- Whether symptoms persist away from work and how they affect attendance, relationships, self-care, and basic functioning
- Job demands, control, support, effort-reward balance, bullying, fairness, insecurity, work hours, and recovery
- Immediate safety concerns, including hopelessness, self-harm thoughts, or inability to function
- Prior medical or mental-health assessment, treatment, leave, workplace adjustments, and confidentiality needs
- Available manager, HR, occupational-health, union, family, medical, and emergency support

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Employee experiencing severe work-related distress
- Manager observing distress across a team
- HR or people professional
- Occupational-health clinician or specialist
- Researcher studying job stress, burnout, and depression

Power asymmetry:

Workers often cannot change demands, control, rewards, bullying, insecurity, or staffing, and disclosure of depression can carry stigma or employment risk. Application should minimize unnecessary diagnostic disclosure to managers, use confidential clinical and occupational-health channels, document conditions and requested changes, and avoid making the employee solely responsible for recovery.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The book draws on international occupational-health and clinical research but uses diagnostic, employment, privacy, accommodation, leave, and treatment concepts that vary by jurisdiction. Depression stigma and the social acceptability of the burnout label also vary across cultures. The authors' proposed occupational-depression construct and Occupational Depression Inventory are not universal legal or clinical standards.

## Core claims

- Author-thesis claim: much severe distress labeled burnout is depressive in nature and the overlap between burnout and depression has been underestimated.
- Author-thesis claim: labeling a depressive condition only as burnout can delay appropriate assessment, treatment, and attention to suicidality.
- Research-scope claim: adverse job conditions, including high demands, low control or support, effort-reward imbalance, and workplace bullying, are associated with depressive symptoms.
- Author-method claim: occupational depression is a more useful construct than burnout for understanding clinically important work-attributed depressive symptoms.
- Intervention-scope claim: affected individuals may need clinical treatment while organizations also change harmful working conditions.
- Boundary claim: the authors' critique is an active scientific position and should not be presented as settled proof that every case called burnout is major depression.

## Decision rules

- If a worker reports persistent exhaustion together with low mood, loss of interest, hopelessness, impaired functioning, or self-harm thoughts, then prioritize qualified clinical assessment rather than assuming a nonclinical burnout state.
- If symptoms are attributed to work, then assess both clinical severity and observable job stressors rather than choosing between individual treatment and organizational change.
- If several workers deteriorate under the same demands, control, reward, support, or bullying conditions, then investigate the shared work environment before attributing the pattern to personal vulnerability.
- If the burnout label reduces shame and opens help-seeking, then use it respectfully as the person's language while clarifying that it does not rule out depression or another condition.
- If a screening score suggests occupational depression, then treat it as a signal for further assessment rather than a standalone diagnosis, unless a qualified clinician using applicable standards reaches a diagnosis.

## Action steps

1. Check immediate safety, severe impairment, and whether urgent professional support is required.
2. Document symptom timing, functional effects, work and nonwork context, and changes over time without assigning a diagnosis.
3. Map demands, control, support, effort and reward, bullying, fairness, insecurity, hours, and recovery using specific events.
4. Use a confidential qualified clinician or occupational-health channel for assessment and treatment planning.
5. Request concrete work changes such as workload reduction, leave, anti-bullying intervention, schedule recovery, support, or role adjustment.
6. Track both health outcomes and changes in working conditions at an agreed review point.
7. Escalate through medical, occupational-health, HR, union, legal, regulatory, or exit routes if safety, impairment, retaliation, or harmful conditions persist.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Employee to a clinician or occupational-health professional, confidential appointment: "These symptoms began or worsened during this work period, persist with this frequency, and affect these functions. I have been calling it burnout, but I would like a proper assessment rather than assume the label is sufficient."
- Employee to manager, written workload request without unnecessary diagnosis disclosure: "The current workload and recovery pattern are affecting my ability to function safely and consistently. I am requesting a review of X, Y, and Z and a written adjustment plan by this date."
- Manager to HR or occupational health after a team pattern appears: "Multiple employees show serious strain under the same conditions. Please review workload, control, support, reward, bullying risk, and confidential care pathways rather than treating this only as individual resilience."

## Counterexamples

- Temporary fatigue after a bounded deadline does not by itself establish depression.
- A person can meet criteria for depression even when no single workplace cause is identifiable.
- A worker may use the burnout label accurately for an occupational experience without having major depressive disorder.
- Changing workload alone may not resolve symptoms that require clinical treatment or are driven by bullying, trauma, physical illness, or nonwork stressors.
- Clinical treatment alone may not produce durable recovery when the employee returns to unchanged harmful conditions.

## Contraindications

- Self-diagnosis, employer diagnosis, or treatment decisions based only on the book or a screening instrument
- Suicidal thoughts, self-harm risk, psychosis, inability to perform basic functions, or another acute emergency requiring immediate qualified support
- Use of the authors' thesis to invalidate a person's preferred language, shame them, or disclose health information unnecessarily
- Use of an occupational-depression label as proof of legal causation, disability status, employer liability, or entitlement
- Advice that delays clinical care or substitutes wellness programs for correction of bullying, unsafe work, or chronic overload

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: The authors have a sustained scholarly commitment to the occupational-depression construct, critique burnout as distinct from depression, and co-developed the Occupational Depression Inventory reproduced in the book. This expertise strengthens the synthesis but creates an intellectual and instrument-development interest in favor of their framework. The book's subtitle deliberately advances a contested thesis.
- Rule conflicts: ['The central thesis conflicts with frameworks that treat burnout as a distinct occupational phenomenon rather than depression.', 'The Occupational Depression Inventory and work-attribution model do not replace established diagnostic interviews or jurisdiction-specific clinical standards.', 'Clinical framing can individualize distress if employers use it to avoid correcting work design, bullying, or labor conditions.', 'Organizational causation claims can exceed what cross-sectional self-report data establish; reverse causality and common-method bias remain relevant.', "Employers' need to manage safety and attendance can conflict with employee privacy and protection from stigma or discrimination."]
- Copyright scope: Safe use includes bibliographic facts, the publisher's chapter titles and description, high-level scientific claims, and original paraphrases with clear attribution. Do not reproduce the book, sample chapter, appendix, Occupational Depression Inventory items or scoring, tables, figures, or substantial passages.
