## Required user information

- The emotion being experienced, its intensity, duration, triggers, and effect on functioning
- The observable workplace event and what remains interpretation rather than fact
- The user's role, the other person's authority, and the consequences of disclosure
- The user's desired outcome, boundary, and preferred working relationship
- The workplace's psychological safety, confidentiality, and history of retaliation or stigma
- Whether the issue reflects ordinary frustration, burnout, grief, harassment, discrimination, trauma, or a health condition
- Which channel, audience, timing, and level of personal disclosure are appropriate

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Employee navigating emotion, belonging, conflict, or work-life boundaries
- Manager responding to an employee's emotions
- Team lead shaping communication norms
- HR or people professional supporting workplace wellbeing
- Executive modeling selective vulnerability
- Remote or hybrid worker managing isolation and communication ambiguity

Power asymmetry:

Emotional expression is judged differently by role, gender, race, disability, culture, and status. A manager's vulnerability may build trust, while a lower-power employee's disclosure may affect reputation, assignments, or perceived reliability. Managers should not require personal disclosure, use emotions as evidence of incompetence, or make employees caretake the leader. Employees should choose the smallest useful disclosure and preserve formal options where retaliation is possible.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The book focuses on modern American professional workplaces. Norms for emotional display, privacy, hierarchy, gender, face-saving, friendship at work, and work-life boundaries vary widely. Local disability, leave, privacy, harassment, discrimination, occupational-health, and recording rules control high-stakes situations.

## Core claims

- Publisher-scope claim: suppressing all workplace emotion can harm health and productivity, while unregulated expression can also damage work and relationships.
- Author-framework claim: emotional effectiveness requires deciding which feelings to discard, contain, examine, or express.
- Author-framework claim: selective vulnerability means being honest enough to be human without making colleagues responsible for one's deepest distress.
- Author-framework claim: feelings provide information but are not automatically facts about another person's motives or the situation.
- Author-framework claim: emotion can be discussed with clear, measured language rather than enacted through accusation, withdrawal, or escalation.
- Author-framework claim: sustainable work may require less identity fusion and less pressure to display constant passion.
- Boundary claim: emotional tools do not replace mental-health care, formal reporting, accommodation, or safety action.

## Decision rules

- If an emotion is intense, then pause long enough to separate the triggering event, bodily response, interpretation, and desired action before communicating.
- If the feeling points to a work problem that another person can address, then share the smallest useful amount of emotion together with facts and a concrete request.
- If disclosure would burden a direct report or require them to comfort the manager, then reduce or redirect it to an appropriate peer, coach, or professional.
- If a message assumes motives, then restate it using observable behavior and acknowledge that the interpretation may be incomplete.
- If a boundary problem is recurring, then communicate capacity, impact, options, and a review point rather than relying on hints or resentment.
- If symptoms are severe, persistent, or impairing, then seek qualified medical or mental-health support and workplace accommodations where appropriate.
- If harassment, discrimination, threats, or retaliation are involved, then prioritize evidence, safety, and formal support over emotional openness.

## Action steps

1. Name the emotion precisely and rate its intensity and urgency.
2. Write down the observable event separately from the story being told about it.
3. Identify the need or value signaled by the emotion and the workplace outcome sought.
4. Assess power, safety, privacy, cultural expectations, and the cost of disclosure.
5. Choose whether to process privately, seek support, set a boundary, ask a question, or make a formal report.
6. Prepare a concise message using facts, impact, limited emotional context, and a specific request.
7. Select a private and timely channel and avoid sending while highly activated.
8. Observe the response and document agreements or concerning conduct.
9. Escalate to qualified support if the issue persists, retaliation appears, or health and safety are affected.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Employee to manager, private capacity conversation: "I am feeling overloaded, and the factual issue is that three urgent projects now share the same Friday deadline. I can complete A and B to standard, or complete all three by Tuesday. Which tradeoff should I use?"
- Manager to team, brief acknowledgment during uncertainty: "I do not have every answer, and I know the reorganization is creating anxiety. What I can confirm today is the decision timeline and that no role changes will be announced before Friday. Please send process questions to me; you are not expected to manage my feelings about it."
- Employee to colleague, private conflict repair: "When the client correction was posted in the group channel before you spoke with me, I felt embarrassed and became defensive. I may be missing context. Next time, please message me first unless the client risk requires an immediate public correction."

## Counterexamples

- Selective vulnerability can still feel unsafe or manipulative when a powerful leader controls consequences.
- Treating feelings as non-facts can be misused to dismiss accurate perceptions of bias, hostility, or danger.
- Emotional openness can expose an employee to stigma in an unsupportive workplace.
- A calm boundary request cannot fix structurally impossible workload or chronic understaffing.
- Reducing passion may be healthy for one worker but conflict with a vocation freely chosen and sustainably practiced by another.
- Humor and illustrations can make guidance accessible but may understate the seriousness of trauma, disability, grief, or abuse.

## Contraindications

- Acute mental-health crisis, self-harm risk, severe functional impairment, or medical symptoms requiring qualified care
- Violence, threats, stalking, harassment, discrimination, or retaliation requiring safety and formal support
- Compelled personal disclosure by a manager, HR, or team
- Use of emotional language to excuse misconduct, unsafe behavior, or failure to meet a legitimate standard
- Assuming that an individual coping strategy can solve chronic overwork, inequity, or a toxic system

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: The authors sell books, speaking, consulting, illustrations, and workplace-emotion content. Their accessible professional-workplace perspective may underrepresent severe mental illness, frontline labor, non-Western display rules, economic precarity, disability, and workplaces where emotional disclosure is unsafe.
- Rule conflicts: ['Authenticity can conflict with privacy, professionalism, or the need to prevent emotional burden from flowing downward.', 'Emotion labeling and reflection can conflict with urgent action during danger, misconduct, or a formal deadline.', 'Manager vulnerability can conflict with role clarity when employees feel required to reassure the manager.', 'Advice to discuss feelings can conflict with legal or investigative guidance about witnesses and evidence.', 'Individual boundary tools can conflict with employer demands or essential duties, requiring negotiation or formal accommodation.']
- Copyright scope: Safe use includes bibliographic facts, general emotional-management principles, and independently written examples. Do not reproduce the book's illustrations, cartoons, chapter structure, worksheets, sample pages, distinctive phrases beyond brief labels, or extended passages.
