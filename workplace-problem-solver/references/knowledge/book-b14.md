## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Executive or manager designing team norms and management systems
- Employee experiencing or observing disrespectful conduct
- Upstander or colleague
- HR, people, compliance, or inclusion professional
- Team facilitator, coach, or organizational adviser

Power asymmetry:

The book explicitly addresses how power amplifies bias, prejudice, and bullying and urges leaders to create checks and balances. Even so, direct scripts are not equally safe for all roles. A low-power employee facing retaliation may need confidential advice, allies, documentation, or formal reporting before confrontation, while a leader has a stronger duty to act and redesign systems.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The framework grows mainly from United States professional workplaces, technology-company experience, and United States discrimination and harassment discourse. Definitions, reporting duties, protected characteristics, investigation rules, privacy, retaliation protections, and collective representation vary by jurisdiction. Direct correction and individual speaking-up scripts also require adaptation in high-power-distance, collectivist, precarious, or unionized settings.

## Core claims

- Author-framework claim: radically respectful workplaces optimize for collaboration rather than coercion and honor individuality rather than demand conformity.
- Author-framework claim: bias, prejudice, and bullying are different problems and require different responses.
- Author-framework claim: when power and management systems amplify harmful conduct, individual problems can become discrimination, harassment, and physical violations.
- Author-practice claim: leaders, upstanders, people harmed, and people who cause harm each have a role, but leaders carry special responsibility for systems and checks on power.
- Author-practice claim: precise diagnosis, shared language, fair process, and management-system design are more useful than treating every harmful workplace as one undifferentiated toxic problem.
- Boundary claim: the framework is practical guidance, not a substitute for a qualified investigation, trauma-informed support, or jurisdiction-specific legal advice.

## Decision rules

- If a statement appears to reflect an unexamined assumption, then use a factual or perspective-based correction when safe, unless repetition, hostility, or context indicates a stronger problem.
- If a person consciously asserts a demeaning belief or crosses a known boundary, then state the boundary and expected conduct clearly, unless direct engagement creates material safety or retaliation risk.
- If conduct is used to dominate, humiliate, or intimidate, then reduce the bully's audience and power, seek allies, document the pattern, and use accountable authority rather than relying only on persuasion.
- If a leader learns of a recurring pattern, then review incentives, decision rights, reporting channels, investigations, and consequences rather than treating each event as an isolated interpersonal misunderstanding.
- If allegations involve discrimination, harassment, violence, retaliation, or protected reporting, then preserve evidence and use qualified formal channels rather than relying solely on the book's conversational tools.

## Action steps

1. Record the observable event, context, impact, roles, and evidence without assigning an unverified motive.
2. Check immediate safety, confidentiality, retaliation exposure, and any formal reporting or legal deadline.
3. Classify the working hypothesis while allowing that bias, prejudice, bullying, and systemic harm can overlap.
4. Choose a role-appropriate, reversible response: factual correction, boundary, interruption, ally, manager intervention, or confidential report.
5. Ask the responsible leader to review the management system, decision rights, incentives, and prior patterns.
6. Document the response, agreed action, owner, deadline, and protection against retaliation.
7. Escalate to HR, compliance, a union, qualified counsel, a regulator, or emergency support when severity or institutional failure requires it.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Employee to colleague, in the moment when safe: "I may be missing your intent, but that statement relies on an assumption about me. Please address the work or decision criteria directly."
- Upstander in a meeting: "I want to pause. The comment moved from disagreement about the proposal to a remark about the person. Can we return to the evidence and the same standard for everyone?"
- Employee to manager or HR, private written report: "On these dates, the following words or actions occurred, with these witnesses and work impacts. I am requesting a confidential review, protection from retaliation, and written confirmation of the process and next update."
- Leader to team after a substantiated pattern: "We are changing both the behavior expectation and the system that allowed this pattern. Here are the decision checks, reporting route, review owner, and consequences going forward."

## Counterexamples

- A single awkward comment can be corrected without proving prejudice, bullying, or an unlawful act.
- An apparently unconscious mistake may be part of a repeated pattern that requires a stronger response than an invitation to learn.
- Directly naming a powerful person's conduct can increase retaliation risk where reporting systems are not trustworthy.
- A respectful conversation cannot repair discriminatory pay, promotion, assignment, or termination without correcting the underlying decision and remedy.
- A code word or shared vocabulary can become performative if leaders do not investigate patterns or change incentives and consequences.

## Contraindications

- Immediate danger, physical assault, threat, stalking, or severe harassment requiring safety and professional support
- Formal investigation, privileged legal review, or confidential complaint process that restricts informal disclosure
- Use of a conversational script as proof that discrimination, harassment, or retaliation did or did not occur
- Pressure on a harmed person to educate, confront, forgive, or mediate with the person accused
- Substitution of the framework for current law, union rights, trauma-informed care, or qualified investigative practice

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: Scott writes from a senior technology-management and executive-coaching perspective and co-founded a company that sells talks, workshops, and implementation support based on her books. This creates commercial and framework-advocacy interests. Her solutions may overrepresent professional-office environments, individual scripts, and leader-led system redesign while underweighting unions, labor enforcement, material remedies, and precarious work.
- Rule conflicts: ['Informal correction and invitation can conflict with formal reporting duties or the need to prevent evidence contamination.', 'Collaboration and repair can conflict with accountability, separation, discipline, or legal remedy after severe or repeated harm.', "Shared workplace language can conflict with employees' culturally different interpretations and freedom to describe their own experience.", 'Leader-designed checks can conflict with independent worker representation or external enforcement when leaders are implicated.', 'The bias-prejudice-bullying classification may not map directly onto legal elements in a particular jurisdiction.']
- Copyright scope: Safe use includes bibliographic facts, high-level public framework terms, original decision rules, and independently written scripts. Do not reproduce the publisher excerpt, the book's stories, diagrams, chapter text, exercises, book-club materials, or substantial passages.
