## Required user information

- The formal performance standards, rating scale, review period, and decision owner
- The employee's agreed goals, actual outputs, constraints, feedback history, and available evidence
- Whether the issue concerns development, compensation, promotion, discipline, a performance improvement plan, or termination
- The degree of manager discretion, calibration practices, peer comparison, and possible bias
- The support, resources, authority, and cross-functional dependencies available to the employee
- The applicable policy, contract, collective agreement, local law, and deadline for responding

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Employee preparing for a performance conversation
- Manager evaluating or developing employees
- HR or people operations professional
- Executive designing a performance system
- Cross-functional leader aligning shared goals

Power asymmetry:

Performance systems give managers and organizations substantial control over ratings, pay, promotion, assignments, and continued employment. Employees need transparent criteria, written examples, and safe correction routes. Managers should not use informal development advice to bypass notice, documentation, consistency, or due process when adverse action is possible.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The collection is primarily grounded in large professional organizations and United States management practice. Rating systems, employee consultation, privacy, works councils, unions, probation, performance improvement plans, dismissal standards, and legal remedies vary across jurisdictions. Direct feedback and individual accountability also require adaptation in high-power-distance and collectivist cultures.

## Core claims

- Publisher-scope claim: conventional annual performance management often falls short and can be redesigned to be more adaptable, developmental, fair, and collaborative.
- Collection-scope claim: performance evaluation should not rely on a single annual event when regular goals, work conditions, and development needs change throughout the year.
- Collection-scope claim: bias, manager perspective, and weak measurement can distort ratings, so evidence, calibration, and transparent standards matter.
- Collection-scope claim: performance systems can unintentionally damage collaboration when individual metrics conflict with shared work.
- Collection-scope claim: numerical metrics and people analytics are useful but incomplete and can create harmful behavior when treated as the whole truth.
- Collection-scope claim: a manager's expectations and behavior can contribute to a self-reinforcing decline in an employee's performance.

## Decision rules

- If a rating is disputed, then compare the written standard, agreed goals, contemporaneous evidence, constraints, and comparable cases before debating motives, unless an urgent legal or retaliation risk requires immediate professional support.
- If the employee first learns of serious deficiencies at the annual review, then treat the feedback process as potentially defective and request specific prior examples and expected corrections, unless the conduct was newly discovered or could not reasonably have been disclosed earlier.
- If an individual metric rewards behavior that harms team outcomes, then add a collaboration or shared-result measure before using the metric for a high-stakes decision, unless the role is genuinely independent.
- If a manager believes an employee is underperforming, then test whether reduced trust, reduced support, vague goals, or constrained authority are reinforcing the problem before escalating discipline.
- If a performance improvement plan may affect employment, then separate developmental coaching from formal legal and policy requirements, unless local qualified advice confirms they can safely be combined.

## Action steps

1. Identify the exact decision at stake and the deadline for influencing it.
2. Collect goals, role expectations, dated feedback, work products, dependencies, and measurable outcomes.
3. Separate observable performance gaps from rating labels, personality judgments, and unsupported comparisons.
4. Check whether standards were communicated in advance and applied consistently.
5. Hold a two-way conversation about evidence, missing context, needed support, and the next review point.
6. Write an agreed plan with outcomes, owners, resources, dates, and how success will be measured.
7. Use calibration, HR review, appeal, union, legal, or other formal channels when the consequence is high or informal correction fails.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Employee to manager, private meeting before rating finalization: "I want to understand the rating against the stated standard. My records show these three outcomes, and these two dependencies changed during the period. Which specific evidence supports the gap, and what evidence would change the assessment?"
- Manager to employee, private meeting soon after a pattern appears: "The agreed result was X. On these dates, the delivered result was Y, which affected Z. What context or constraint am I missing? Let us define the support, next outcome, and review date in writing."
- Manager to HR or calibration group, written review before an adverse decision: "Please test whether this proposed rating uses the same evidence threshold as comparable cases and whether role scope, resources, and cross-functional dependencies have been considered."

## Counterexamples

- Replacing annual reviews with frequent check-ins does not improve fairness if the check-ins remain vague, undocumented, or retaliatory.
- A quantitative target can be valid and necessary in a regulated or production role; the problem is not measurement itself but incomplete or manipulable measurement.
- An employee's weak result is not always caused by the manager or system; clear standards, adequate support, and repeated evidence may show a genuine capability or conduct gap.
- Team metrics can conceal individual nonperformance when ownership is unclear.
- Developmental language can become misleading when the organization has already decided to terminate employment.

## Contraindications

- Immediate termination, forced resignation, discrimination, retaliation, wage loss, or legal deadlines requiring qualified local advice
- Use of the collection as a substitute for applicable labor law, policy, contract, collective agreement, or formal investigation
- Use of subjective frameworks to justify an adverse decision without prior standards and evidence
- Mental-health diagnosis or assumptions that health conditions explain performance

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: Harvard Business Review Press sells the book and related learning products. The collection is edited for managerial usefulness and primarily reflects employers, managers, consultants, and large professional organizations. It may underweight worker rights, unions, frontline settings, small firms, and jurisdictions with stronger procedural protections.
- Rule conflicts: ['Frequent informal feedback can conflict with privacy, works council, union, or formal documentation requirements.', 'Manager-discretion models can conflict with demands for standardized, auditable, and legally defensible evaluation.', 'Strengths-oriented job sculpting can conflict with essential job duties and resource constraints.', 'Collaboration metrics can conflict with individual accountability when ownership is not visible.', 'A development-first approach can conflict with legal advice to preserve evidence and limit informal statements during a dispute.']
- Copyright scope: Safe use includes bibliographic facts, article titles, high-level themes, and independently written paraphrases with article-level attribution. Do not reproduce the collection, complete articles, charts, assessment tools, cases, or substantial passages.
