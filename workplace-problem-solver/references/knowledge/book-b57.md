## Required user information

- Whether the group is stable or fluid and how frequently membership changes
- The task's uncertainty, complexity, interdependence, novelty, and consequences of failure
- Who holds relevant expertise, local knowledge, decision authority, and operational information
- The current level of speaking up, reflection, experimentation, information sharing, and psychological safety
- The incentives, hierarchy, professional boundaries, and organizational barriers affecting collaboration
- Which failures are preventable, complexity-related, or intelligent experiments

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Cross-functional leader
- Project or program manager
- Manager of changing or temporary teams
- Executive leading organizational learning
- Professional in healthcare, operations, innovation, or other complex work
- Team member coordinating across expertise boundaries

Power asymmetry:

Dynamic collaboration often joins people with different status, professions, organizational homes, and decision rights. Lower-status expertise may remain silent unless leaders explicitly invite it and respond productively. Psychological safety does not remove accountability or retaliation risk; reporting and escalation safeguards still matter.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The research and cases span several United States organizations and industries, including healthcare, manufacturing, government, and design. Professional hierarchy, error reporting, privacy, regulation, language, and face-saving norms vary across cultures and sectors. Adapt the practices without assuming that open speaking alone is safe or lawful.

## Core claims

- Publisher-scope claim: modern work increasingly depends on flexible, fluid collaborations rather than only stable teams with time to develop routines.
- Author-framework claim: teaming is an active process of coordinating and learning while work is underway.
- Author-framework claim: interpersonal fear, groupthink, power dynamics, information hoarding, and mistaken beliefs about failure can block learning.
- Author-framework claim: reflection, psychological safety, speaking up, experimentation, and cross-boundary collaboration support organizational learning.
- Author-framework claim: learning practices should differ according to the uncertainty and routine character of the work.
- Boundary claim: psychological safety enables candor but does not guarantee technical quality, ethical conduct, or good decisions.

## Decision rules

- If work is novel, complex, and performed by changing contributors, then establish rapid orientation, shared purpose, expertise mapping, and learning loops rather than waiting for the group to stabilize.
- If a high-status person speaks first on an uncertain issue, then solicit independent input from relevant lower-status experts before converging, unless emergency command requires immediate direction.
- If a failure occurs, then classify whether it was preventable, complexity-related, or an intelligent experiment before assigning response and accountability.
- If people remain silent despite invitations, then examine retaliation history, hierarchy, incentives, and leader reactions rather than concluding that there are no concerns.
- If the task is routine and well understood, then use reliable execution and process discipline rather than unnecessary experimentation.

## Action steps

1. Classify the work by uncertainty, interdependence, novelty, and consequence.
2. Create a short shared purpose and identify expertise, decision rights, and information gaps.
3. Use structured introductions and rapid onboarding when membership changes.
4. Invite questions, concerns, and local knowledge before leaders announce a preferred answer.
5. Run brief reflection cycles after milestones, surprises, successes, and failures.
6. Convert learning into an updated procedure, decision, experiment, or escalation.
7. Track whether speaking up produces improvement or retaliation and strengthen safeguards accordingly.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Cross-functional lead at project start: "This group will change over time, so let us make the purpose, expertise, decision rights, and current unknowns visible before we assign work."
- Senior leader before a high-uncertainty decision: "I have a view, but I do not want it to anchor the room. Please hear the frontline and specialist assessments first, including evidence that challenges the current plan."
- Team lead after a failure: "Before assigning blame, we will determine whether this was a known-process violation, a complexity surprise, or a bounded experiment, then decide the corrective action and accountability."

## Counterexamples

- A stable routine team may need standard work and reliability more than continuous experimentation.
- Inviting dissent publicly can increase risk when retaliation is likely and confidential channels are absent.
- Treating every failure as learning can excuse preventable negligence or repeated disregard of safeguards.
- Psychological safety without clear standards can produce comfortable discussion but weak execution.
- Cross-functional participation can become meeting overload when decision rights and required expertise are unclear.

## Contraindications

- Immediate emergency command where delay would create greater harm
- Formal investigation, protected disclosure, or confidential incident review requiring controlled communication
- Use of learning language to excuse fraud, harassment, reckless conduct, or repeated preventable violations
- Experiments involving people, safety, privacy, or regulated activity without appropriate ethics and compliance controls

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: Edmondson is an academic researcher who also publishes trade books, teaches executive education, consults, and participates in paid speaking and learning programs. The teaming lens may foreground learning and collaboration while underweighting labor conflict, distributive power, staffing, or formal governance.
- Rule conflicts: ['Experimentation and intelligent failure can conflict with safety, regulation, privacy, and zero-defect requirements.', 'Open reflection can conflict with legal privilege, investigations, confidentiality, or fear of retaliation.', 'Fluid teaming can conflict with role stability, professional licensing, workload protection, and clear accountability.', 'Psychological safety interventions can conflict with approaches that prioritize structural power and enforceable worker protections.']
- Copyright scope: Safe use includes bibliographic facts, high-level public concepts such as teaming and psychological safety, and independently written paraphrases of learning practices. Do not reproduce chapters, case narratives, figures, assessments, or substantial portions of official excerpts.
