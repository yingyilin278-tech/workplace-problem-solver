## Required user information

- The expected performance, evidence of the actual gap, and whether standards were communicated in advance
- The employee's perspective on ability, clarity, resources, workload, autonomy, fairness, meaning, recognition, and growth
- The manager's authority over job design, pay, workload, rewards, development, and staffing
- Previous feedback, support, recognition, consequences, and their observed effects
- Whether the issue is individual, team-wide, structural, discriminatory, health-related, or a formal performance matter
- Relevant compensation rules, collective agreements, accommodations, and local employment procedures

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- People manager
- Team leader
- Executive
- HR or talent professional
- Project lead with limited formal authority

Power asymmetry:

Managers control assignments, evaluations, recognition, development, and often pay or job security. They must not use motivational techniques to conceal coercion, impossible workload, unequal treatment, or predetermined discipline. Employees need clear standards, voice, consistent process, and a safe route to challenge unfair treatment.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The guide reflects mainstream United States management practice. The meaning of recognition, individual rewards, public praise, purpose, autonomy, performance management, working hours, and employee consultation varies by culture, jurisdiction, sector, and collective agreement. Local policy and law govern compensation and employment decisions.

## Core claims

- Publisher-scope claim: low performance should be diagnosed by its root causes rather than treated automatically as a lack of motivation.
- Publisher-scope claim: managers can improve engagement by understanding what drains employees and by connecting work to meaning and strengths.
- Publisher-scope claim: recognition and rewards should account for individual differences rather than assuming one incentive fits everyone.
- Publisher-scope claim: growth and development support are important parts of sustained motivation.
- Publisher-scope claim: preventing burnout, especially among strong performers, is a management responsibility rather than only an employee self-care task.
- Boundary claim: managerial techniques cannot compensate for unfair pay, unsafe work, discrimination, chronic understaffing, or lack of legitimate authority.

## Decision rules

- If performance declines, then check goal clarity, capability, resources, workload, autonomy, fairness, health, and consequences before labeling the employee unmotivated.
- If an employee values a particular form of recognition, then use it consistently and proportionately, unless it conflicts with fairness, privacy, policy, or the person's stated preference.
- If top performers repeatedly receive more work because they are reliable, then rebalance capacity and opportunity before adding further stretch goals.
- If routine work feels disconnected from purpose, then explain the real beneficiary and outcome without inventing a noble story or hiding poor conditions.
- If the issue may require formal performance action, then separate supportive diagnosis from evaluation and follow documented standards and review procedures.

## Action steps

1. State the expected result, evidence, time period, and business impact.
2. Ask the employee what obstacles they see across clarity, skill, resources, workload, autonomy, fairness, and well-being.
3. Verify the explanation with workload data, prior standards, access, and comparable treatment.
4. Choose the smallest appropriate intervention: clarify, train, resource, redesign, recognize, rebalance, or formally address behavior.
5. Agree on the action, owner, support, measurable result, and review date.
6. Check for unequal workload, biased recognition, burnout risk, or team-wide structural causes.
7. Use formal HR, legal, accommodation, safety, or employee-representation channels when the issue exceeds ordinary coaching.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Manager to employee, private diagnostic conversation: "The expected result was X, and the current evidence is Y. Before deciding why, I want to understand whether the main constraint is clarity, skill, access, workload, or something else. What am I missing?"
- Manager to a strong performer before assigning more work: "You have delivered consistently, and I do not want reliability to become overload. If you take X, which responsibility should move, and what development value should this assignment provide?"
- Manager giving recognition in the employee's preferred channel: "Your action on X changed Y for the customer and team. I want to recognize the specific contribution and also confirm that the workload required was sustainable."

## Counterexamples

- A bonus can reduce trust when the employee's real problem is unfair workload, poor management, or unclear standards.
- Public praise may embarrass an employee or expose them to resentment when they prefer private recognition.
- Purpose messaging can feel manipulative when pay, safety, staffing, or product ethics remain unresolved.
- Autonomy without training, access, or decision authority is abandonment rather than motivation.
- Coaching cannot resolve intentional misconduct, serious capability gaps, or formal policy violations by itself.

## Contraindications

- Use of motivational tactics to avoid fair pay, adequate staffing, safe work, accommodation, or lawful working-time obligations
- Discrimination, harassment, retaliation, violence, or whistleblowing matters requiring protected procedures
- Formal discipline, dismissal, pay reduction, or promotion decisions without evidence and due process
- Rewards that pressure employees to conceal errors, compete destructively, work unpaid hours, or violate policy
- Clinical or medical interpretation of burnout, depression, disability, or health disclosures

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: Harvard Business Review Press sells the guide, related collections, and a paid toolkit. The manager-centered framing may overestimate a manager's discretion and underweight labor rights, collective bargaining, compensation inequality, discrimination, organizational design, and employee resistance to managerial control.
- Rule conflicts: ['Individualized rewards can conflict with pay equity, consistency, transparency, and team cooperation.', "Manager-created purpose can conflict with employees' own values or with ethically contested organizational goals.", 'Strengths-based assignments can pigeonhole employees or deny access to development outside established strengths.', 'Engagement goals can conflict with the legitimate choice to maintain emotional distance and work only to agreed standards.', 'Informal motivation practices can conflict with formal performance, accommodation, and collective-agreement procedures.']
- Copyright scope: Safe use includes bibliographic facts, publisher-listed themes, and independently written diagnostic and management rules. Do not reproduce articles, chapters, cases, questionnaires, toolkit templates, or substantial passages.
