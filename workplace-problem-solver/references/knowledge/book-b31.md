## Required user information

- The review period, employee role, agreed goals, job-relevant competencies, and rating definitions
- Dated evidence of outcomes, behavior, quality, collaboration, customer impact, and constraints across the full period
- Prior feedback, coaching, support, resources, changed priorities, and the employee's responses
- The employee's self-assessment and any material factual disagreement
- Comparable standards and calibration data used for similarly situated employees
- The review's consequences for pay, promotion, development, discipline, continued employment, or documentation
- Possible bias, protected leave, disability, accommodation, discrimination, retaliation, or collective-agreement implications
- Who has authority to rate, approve, calibrate, correct, and retain the review record

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Manager conducting a formal review
- Employee preparing a self-assessment or responding to a review
- HR professional designing, calibrating, or auditing review processes
- Executive accountable for performance-management fairness
- People manager setting goals and development plans

Power asymmetry:

A formal review can affect compensation, promotion, assignments, immigration sponsorship, discipline, and termination. The manager therefore bears a high burden to use job-related standards, representative evidence, consistent process, and a meaningful opportunity for response. The employee may need access to the written review, correction channels, representation, or an appeal. A development conversation should not be used to conceal a predetermined adverse decision.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

The guide reflects general United States managerial practice. Legal requirements for personnel records, consultation, works councils, unions, ratings, notice, discrimination, privacy, retention, and dismissal vary. Cultural norms affect self-promotion, disagreement with a manager, and interpretation of ratings. Local law, policy, and collective agreements override the primer.

## Core claims

- Publisher-scope claim: performance reviews formally compare accomplishments with goals and can support employee development.
- Publisher-scope claim: preparation requires gathering and analyzing the right information rather than relying on memory.
- Publisher-scope claim: managers should document their assessment and address performance problems directly.
- Publisher-scope claim: the conversation should recognize achievements and clarify development needs.
- Publisher-scope claim: a useful review establishes challenging, relevant goals for the next period.
- Practice-scope claim: review quality improves when ongoing feedback and evidence precede the formal meeting.
- Boundary claim: a short review guide cannot establish legal fairness, eliminate evaluator bias, or validate an organization's rating system.

## Decision rules

- If a rating cannot be supported with dated job-relevant evidence from the review period, then do not finalize it until the evidence is checked.
- If goals changed during the period, then assess against the documented revised expectations rather than the obsolete plan.
- If one recent event dominates the assessment, then review the full period and representative outcomes before assigning a rating.
- If the employee disputes a material fact, then record the disagreement and verify the evidence through the defined process before using it for a high-stakes decision.
- If a performance gap was never communicated and no urgent misconduct is involved, then provide clear feedback and reasonable support before treating the review as a surprise sanction.
- If different groups or managers apply standards inconsistently, then calibrate evidence and criteria without forcing an artificial distribution.
- If the review may lead to discipline, termination, discrimination risk, accommodation issues, or retaliation concerns, then follow formal policy and qualified local advice.

## Action steps

1. Confirm the review period, role expectations, goals, rating scale, approval path, and consequences.
2. Collect representative evidence from the full period, including outcomes, behaviors, prior feedback, changed priorities, and support provided.
3. Invite the employee's self-assessment and check factual differences before drafting conclusions.
4. Audit the draft for recency, proximity, affinity, gender, race, disability, caregiving, and visibility bias.
5. Compare standards across similarly situated employees through a documented calibration process.
6. Draft an assessment that separates evidence, evaluation, impact, and future expectations.
7. Conduct a private two-way conversation, allow a response, and distinguish recognition, development, and any formal performance issue.
8. Agree on a small number of measurable next-period goals, support, checkpoints, and owners.
9. Provide the approved record, correction or appeal route, and follow-up dates according to policy.
10. Monitor the goals and give ongoing feedback so the next formal review contains no avoidable surprises.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Manager opening a formal review, private scheduled meeting: "This review covers January through December and uses the goals and standards we confirmed in February and revised in July. I will summarize the evidence and assessment, then I want your view on anything missing or inaccurate before we agree on next steps."
- Manager addressing a performance gap: "The agreed target was a complete cost validation before release. Three of twelve quotes required correction, including two after client review. We discussed this on September 8 and introduced a checklist on September 12. The next step is 100 percent checklist completion and manager review for the next five quotes, with a progress check in two weeks."
- Employee disputing a review in writing: "I disagree with the statement that the deadline was missed without notice. I notified the project lead on March 4 and attached that message here. Please add this evidence to the record and confirm the process and date for correcting the factual section."

## Counterexamples

- A well-run meeting cannot repair a rating system that rewards visibility, similarity to the manager, or access to unequal opportunities.
- Numerical ratings can create false precision and encourage forced comparisons unsupported by evidence.
- Stretch goals can become punitive when resources, authority, dependencies, or business conditions make them unrealistic.
- An annual review is too late to be the first time an employee hears about a recurring gap.
- Calibration can reduce inconsistency but can also institutionalize group bias or become disguised stack ranking.
- Documentation can be accurate yet unfair if the underlying goals changed informally or the employee lacked necessary accommodation or support.

## Contraindications

- Use as a substitute for local employment law, collective agreements, formal policy, accommodation, investigation, or legal review
- Ratings or adverse decisions based on protected characteristics, protected leave, retaliation, rumor, or undocumented personality judgments
- Predetermined termination or discipline presented deceptively as an open development conversation
- Forced ranking or quota-based ratings without valid job-related evidence and authorized organizational review
- Disclosure of personnel records beyond authorized participants

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: Harvard Business Review Press sells the guide, related tools, collections, and performance-management content. The manager-centered primer assumes a functioning goal and review process and may underweight employee representation, labor rights, algorithmic bias, disability, discrimination, contested evidence, and structural inequity.
- Rule conflicts: ['Developmental openness can conflict with the procedural requirements of an active disciplinary or legal matter.', "Challenging goals can conflict with reasonable accommodation, workload limits, safety, and dependencies outside the employee's control.", 'Manager judgment can conflict with research showing systematic bias in ratings and narrative evaluations.', 'Calibration can conflict with individualized evidence when leaders impose a distribution rather than test standards.', 'Detailed documentation can conflict with data-minimization, privacy, access, and retention requirements.', 'Formal annual review cycles can conflict with continuous-feedback systems, project-based work, and rapidly changing roles.']
- Copyright scope: Safe use includes bibliographic facts, publisher-listed review tasks, general evidence and goal-setting principles, and independently written examples. Do not reproduce chapters, forms, assessment criteria, worksheets, rating tools, or substantial text from the book or related products.
