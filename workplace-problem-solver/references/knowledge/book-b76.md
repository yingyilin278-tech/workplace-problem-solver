# B76 — The Culture Map

- Source type: Research-based trade book with case studies
- Coverage: C1, C2, C4, C6, C8, C10, C12

Official sources:

## Required user information

- The countries, regions, or organizational cultures involved.
- Whether the issue is communication style, decision making, evaluation, persuasion, leadership, or scheduling.
- The user's role, authority, and stakes in the cross-cultural exchange.
- Time pressure and the cost of misreading cultural cues.
- Whether translation, an interpreter, or a cultural advisor is available.

## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Multinational team members, expats, and global managers.
- Project leaders running cross-regional or cross-functional initiatives.
- HR and learning partners designing global collaboration programs.

Power asymmetry:

The framework helps decode behavior, not excuse bad behavior. It should not be used to justify discrimination, harassment, retaliation, or unlawful conduct under the guise of "cultural difference."

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

Local law, contract, and policy still take priority. The framework is a diagnostic tool, not a license to override mandatory workplace rules.

## Core claims

- National and organizational cultures differ on measurable dimensions such as communication, evaluation, persuasion, leadership, decision making, scheduling, and trust-building.
- A behavior that signals respect in one culture can signal weakness or aggression in another.
- Effective cross-cultural collaboration requires explicit calibration rather than assuming one's own norms are universal.
- Many cross-border conflicts are misdiagnosed as personality or competence issues.

## Decision rules

- If a misunderstanding is recurring across people from the same region, treat it as a likely cultural pattern rather than an individual failure.
- If the other party comes from a high-context culture, prefer explicit written confirmation after a meeting.
- If the user is uncertain about expectations, ask for examples rather than abstract descriptions.
- If a local norm conflicts with the user's home values, identify the non-negotiable points before negotiating the rest.

## Action steps

1. Identify the cultural dimensions most likely affecting the exchange.
2. Map the gap between the user's default style and the counterpart's likely style.
3. Prepare a short written summary of the agreed next step.
4. Ask open questions about expectations and decision criteria.
5. Review the exchange with a culturally fluent colleague or advisor.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Audience: cross-cultural partner; channel: written message; timing: after a meeting. Wording: "我们刚讨论的内容比较多，我整理了一份要点，请确认我的理解是否一致。"
- Audience: team member; channel: one-on-one; timing: early in a project. Wording: "我们做决定的方式可能不一样，可以先告诉我你那边的标准流程是怎样的吗？"

## Counterexamples

- Do not assume every difference is cultural; some are role, seniority, or individual.
- Do not use the framework to excuse clear misconduct.
- Do not over-generalize a country-level pattern to every individual from that country.

## Contraindications

- Discrimination, harassment, or retaliation disguised as "cultural fit."
- Legal or compliance decisions where local law is unambiguous.

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: Author is affiliated with a global business school; framework is widely used in corporate training.
- Rule conflicts: ['Country-level patterns can mask organizational and individual variation.', 'Some practices differ in degree, not in kind.']
