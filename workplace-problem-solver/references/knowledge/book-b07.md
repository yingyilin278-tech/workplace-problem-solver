## Applicable roles, power, and culture

Applicable roles:

- Employees
- Managers
- Job applicants
- Coworkers and project leads
- Workers considering escalation or departure

Power asymmetry:

Matter-of-fact directness is most useful when the other party can respond without severe retaliation. With a boss, HR, or employer, the user should distinguish a routine awkward conversation from a rights, safety, or job-security issue and preserve records before escalation.

Jurisdiction and cultural transfer:

Advice reflects United States workplace conventions and an advice-column perspective. Norms around at-will employment, notice, leave, privacy, unions, hierarchy, and directness vary substantially; legal and policy questions require local verification.

## Decision rules

- If the behavior is ordinary but disruptive and a reasonable person may not know its impact, address it directly and privately before escalating.
- If the request concerns priorities or workload, explain the tradeoff and ask the person with authority to choose.
- If the issue is serious misconduct, pay, discrimination, safety, or formal discipline, preserve evidence and use the relevant policy or professional channel instead of relying only on an interpersonal script.
- If repeated direct conversations produce no change and the organization will not intervene, assess whether adaptation, transfer, or exit is more realistic than another identical conversation.

## Action steps

1. Identify the narrow behavior to change and remove assumptions about motive.
2. Check whether a direct conversation is safe and whether the issue belongs with the coworker, manager, HR, or an external professional.
3. Use a short, normal tone that states the fact, impact, and request.
4. Pause and allow the other person to respond rather than over-explaining.
5. Document the outcome when duties, pay, performance, safety, or future accountability are affected.
6. Escalate or exit when the response demonstrates that the problem is structural or unsafe.

## Ready-to-use wording

- Audience: Coworker
- Channel and timing: Private conversation or direct message; Soon after a repeatable problem occurs

> When the client file changes without a note, I cannot tell which version is final. Please message me when you update it and include the decision owner.

- Audience: Manager
- Channel and timing: One-to-one meeting; When feedback is vague

> I want to turn that feedback into something I can act on. What specific behavior should change, what would good performance look like, and when should we check progress?

## Counterexamples

- Directness can backfire when the user treats an uncertain interpretation as fact or uses a script to prosecute motive.
- A normal conversation is not a sufficient response to violence, severe harassment, wage problems, or an imminent formal employment decision.
- United States advice-column norms may be too informal or individualistic in highly hierarchical, unionized, or regulated workplaces.

## Contraindications

- Immediate safety threats or medical emergencies
- Requests for definitive legal conclusions about pay, discrimination, leave, dismissal, or contracts
- Situations where preserving evidence must precede notifying the alleged wrongdoer
- Severe psychological distress or functional impairment requiring professional care

## Conflicts and limitations

- Author bias and conflicts: The author writes and monetizes workplace advice and consulting. Her former management role provides practical insight, but the viewpoint may privilege conventional United States professional norms and managerial interpretation. Advice-column cases are selected and based on one-sided reports.
- Rule conflicts: The book's pragmatic directness may conflict with labor-law requirements, collective-bargaining procedures, trauma-informed practice, or cultures where private sponsorship and indirect communication matter. Structural discrimination and abusive power cannot be reduced to better phrasing.
- Copyright scope: Paraphrase general principles and create new situation-specific wording. Do not copy the book's large collection of scripts, reproduce full advice entries, or present adapted wording as a quotation from the author.
